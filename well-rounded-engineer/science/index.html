Science and the scientific method

Engineering runs on the same method as science: observe, form a hypothesis, predict, test, and let the result decide. Debugging, performance work, data quality and AI evaluation are all experiments. This tab is about thinking like a scientist at work, and knowing enough about how computers physically work to reason about them.

The method, applied to engineering

StepIn scienceIn engineering
ObserveNotice something unexplainedp95 latency doubled after Tuesday's deploy
HypothesiseA testable explanationThe new query skips the index on store_id
PredictWhat must be true if the hypothesis holdsEXPLAIN will show a sequential scan; reverting the query will restore latency
TestAn experiment that could prove it wrongRun EXPLAIN ANALYZE; try the fix on a copy with production-sized data
ConcludeAccept, reject or refineSequential scan confirmed; index added; p95 back to normal, measured
SharePublish so others can checkPR description with before/after numbers; a regression test

The key word is falsifiable: a good hypothesis says what result would prove it wrong. "Something in the network is flaky" cannot be tested; "the timeouts happen only on calls through the NAT gateway" can.

What science can answer, and what it cannot settle alone

Science is the best method we have for questions about what is true of the world and how it works: questions an observation or an experiment could answer. Keep it for those, and keep a balance. Trying to find every answer in science is the same mistake as trying to find every answer in a religion, or in any single tradition: one tool treated as the whole toolbox.

  • What is, and how it works. Does this index make the query faster? Does this drug lower blood pressure? Observation, measurement and experiment answer these: this is science's home ground.
  • What we ought to do. Should we collect this data at all? Is this model fair to the people it scores? Ethics, law and judgement answer these, informed by evidence about what the consequences will be.
  • What matters, and why. What makes work worth doing? What gives a life meaning? People find these answers in philosophy, faith, tradition, art, relationships and their own experience.
  • An "is" does not give an "ought" on its own. David Hume noticed that arguments slide from what is the case to what should be done without saying how. Data tells you what users do, not what you should build for them. Metrics guide; values decide.
  • Science rests on assumptions it does not prove by its own method: that nature behaves regularly, that instruments and senses are roughly reliable, that results are reported honestly. They are good assumptions, and they are still assumptions.
  • Treating science as the answer to every question has a name, scientism. It is a philosophical position, not a scientific finding.
  • People disagree about how far the domains overlap. Stephen Jay Gould argued that science and religion answer different questions ("non-overlapping magisteria"); others argue they do overlap. You do not need to settle it to work well with people of every view.
  • Respect people whose answers differ from yours. Many careful scientists hold a religious faith and many hold none. A team works best when what is measurable is measured, and what is not is discussed with humility.

Use each kind of knowledge for the questions it can answer, and be honest about where every one of them runs out, including your own favourite.

Debugging as an experiment

  • Reproduce first. A bug you cannot reproduce is a bug you cannot prove you fixed.
  • Change one variable at a time, or you will not know which change mattered.
  • Bisect: halve the search space each step (git bisect, removing half the input, disabling half the config).
  • Write down each hypothesis and its result, including the ones that failed. They narrow the search.
  • Prefer evidence that could surprise you: look at the raw log, the packet, the row, not a summary that agrees with your guess.
  • Stop when the evidence explains everything, not when you have a plausible story.

Measurement

  • Accuracy vs precision: a clock that is consistently 5 minutes fast is precise but not accurate.
  • Know your instrument: what it measures, its resolution, what it misses, and what it reports when it cannot measure.
  • Units on every number. "Latency 200" means nothing.
  • Error bars: run a benchmark several times and report the spread, not one lucky run.
  • Baseline first: measure before the change, in the same conditions, or the comparison is meaningless.
  • The observer effect: profilers, debug logging and tracing change what they measure. Account for it.

Experiments with data and users

  • Control group: compare against something that did not get the change, at the same time.
  • Randomise who gets which variant, so other differences average out.
  • Decide the metric and sample size before you look, or you will find what you hoped for (p-hacking).
  • Confounders: a feature launched in the same week as a holiday cannot be judged on that week alone.
  • Reproducibility: pin data versions, code versions and random seeds so someone else gets the same answer.

How computers physically work

  • CPU: executes instructions in cycles; modern cores run billions per second and work on several instructions at once.
  • Memory hierarchy: registers, then CPU caches (L1, L2, L3), then RAM, then SSD, then network. Each step is roughly 10–1,000 times slower than the one before. Code that reads memory in order is far faster than code that jumps around.
  • Storage: SSDs read randomly far faster than spinning disks, but sequential reads are still fastest.
  • Networks: limited by the speed of light; a round trip across a continent cannot be faster than tens of milliseconds.
  • Energy and heat: compute has a physical cost, which is also a cost in money and carbon.

These physical limits explain most performance rules on the Performance and System Design tabs.

Reading research and technical papers

  1. First pass (minutes): title, abstract, introduction, headings, conclusions. Decide if it is worth more time.
  2. Second pass (an hour): figures, tables, the main argument; note what you do not understand.
  3. Third pass (hours): recreate the work in your head or in code; challenge every assumption.

Ask of any claim: what was measured, on what data, compared with what, and could the result be explained another way?

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Reproduce a bug before fixing it
  • Change one thing at a time and measure before and after
  • Write down hypotheses and results while debugging

2 Practitioner

  • Debug systematically with falsifiable hypotheses and bisection
  • Run fair benchmarks with baselines and error bars
  • Spot common biases in your own reasoning

3 Senior

  • Design controlled experiments and A/B tests correctly
  • Read papers critically and apply their results with care
  • Teach the method through how you run investigations

4 Expert

  • Build a culture of evidence and experiment across the organisation
  • Lead research-style work with rigour and reproducibility
  • Judge competing claims across fields

Each level includes the ones before it. See Start here for what the levels mean.
