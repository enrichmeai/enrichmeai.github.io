Guide / Architecture & design / Case Studies: How Tech Companies Evolved

Case studies: how technology companies evolved

Eight companies, and the turns their engineering took: Yahoo, a pioneer whose engineers helped build Hadoop while its own products lost ground; eBay, which split one auction site by function and by data and gave up distributed transactions to do it; Amazon, whose internal services became Amazon Web Services; Facebook, now Meta, which kept its PHP code and changed the language and runtime beneath it; Netflix, which moved to the cloud after an outage and then made failure part of its testing; GitHub, which kept one large Rails application and changed what was underneath it; Shopify, which split its data across databases; and Stack Overflow, whose biggest change came from outside its architecture. Each case gives what happened, from published sources; our view of it, labelled; another way to see it; what you can take from it, and when not to copy it; and a link to the company’s own account. The patterns behind them are on Microservices, Solution Architecture: A to B and Distributed Systems.

Eight directions

Yahoo: a pioneer that built the industry’s tools and lost its own market

What happened. In January 1994 Jerry Yang and David Filo, graduate students at Stanford, made “Jerry and David’s Guide to the World Wide Web”, a human-edited directory organised in a hierarchy rather than a searchable index. Yahoo was incorporated in March 1995, added a search engine the same year, and became the first popular online directory and search engine on the web. Yahoo began using Google for its search in June 2000; over the next four years it built its own search technology, partly from Inktomi, which it bought for $280 million in 2002, and switched to it in 2004. Its engineers were central to Hadoop, the open-source system for storing and processing data across many machines that grew from Google’s papers: in February 2008 Yahoo launched the Search Webmap, a Hadoop application on more than 10,000 cores whose data was used in every Yahoo web search. The same month Microsoft offered $44.6 billion for the company; Yahoo rejected it. In 2016 Yahoo disclosed two data breaches, one in 2013 affecting over a billion accounts and one in 2014 affecting over 500 million. Verizon announced in July 2016 that it would buy Yahoo’s internet business for $4.83 billion and completed the purchase in June 2017; in 2021 funds managed by Apollo bought 90% of it. Wikipedia sums it up: one of the pioneers of the early internet, whose use declined in the 2010s as it lost market share to Facebook and Google.

Our view: Yahoo’s engineering helped build tools much of the industry came to run on, Hadoop above all, while its own products lost users; good infrastructure did not decide the outcome. The breaches add a second lesson that ties to Quality Attributes: security failures came to light during the company’s sale, years after they happened.

The other view: Yahoo’s decline is usually explained by strategy and leadership more than engineering (Wikipedia describes strategic missteps alongside the security incidents), so it is a weak guide to architecture decisions; it says more about product focus than about systems.

What you can take (analysis): know which part of your work is the product users choose you for, and which is infrastructure others could supply; and treat security as part of what a company is worth, not only a technical risk. Don’t copy it if you are tempted to conclude “build your own search” or “build your own platform”: Yahoo did both, and the case does not show that either decided the outcome.

Sources: Wikipedia’s articles on Yahoo and Apache Hadoop. We could not read a Yahoo engineering account from this environment.

Why Yahoo declined, and is it finished?

No single decision sank Yahoo. What the sources describe is a run of choices over about fifteen years. Wikipedia’s summary: “Yahoo’s decline was punctuated by a series of strategic missteps and severe security incidents.”

WhenThe choiceWhat happened
1998, 2002Did not buy GoogleIn 1998 Larry Page and Sergey Brin offered to sell their search engine to Yahoo for $1 million, and Yahoo declined. In 2002 Terry Semel negotiated to buy Google for a reported $5 billion; Yahoo’s final offer was $3 billion, which Google rejected. Wikipedia: that second miss “is widely considered one of the largest strategic errors in corporate history”.
1999–2001Bought big, at the top of the bubbleYahoo grew into a web portal, a site meant to keep users inside it, and made its two biggest acquisitions in 1999: GeoCities for $3.6 billion and Broadcast.com for $5.7 billion. Its share price closed at $118.75 on 3 January 2000 and fell to $8.11 on 26 September 2001. GeoCities closed in October 2009.
2000–2010Did not keep control of searchGoogle powered Yahoo’s searches from June 2000. Yahoo switched to its own technology in February 2004, and from August 2010 its organic results came from Microsoft’s Bing. In ten years Yahoo rented its search, built its own, then rented it again.
2004–2006Sold trust in its resultsA paid-inclusion programme guaranteed commercial sites a listing for a fee. Marketers resisted the fees, and the public disliked that paid listings could not be told apart from organic results; Yahoo scaled it back by October 2006.
2008Turned down MicrosoftYahoo rejected Microsoft’s offer of $44.6 billion. Microsoft raised it to $47 billion; Yahoo held out for at least 10% more, and Microsoft withdrew in May 2008. Nine years later Verizon paid $4.48 billion for the internet business.
2001–2012Changed leaders again and againTerry Semel (2001), Jerry Yang (2007), Carol Bartz (2009, fired in 2011), Tim Morse (interim), Scott Thompson (January 2012), Ross Levinsohn (interim, May 2012) and Marissa Mayer (July 2012): seven chief executives in about eleven years, each with a new direction. The largest layoff in its history, 2,000 people, came in early 2012.
2013–2017Bought audience, then lost trustUnder Mayer, Yahoo bought Tumblr in 2013 for $1.1 billion in cash. In 2016 it disclosed breaches from 2013 and 2014 covering over a billion and over 500 million accounts. Verizon cut its price by $350 million because of them, completing the purchase in June 2017.

The best deal it made. In August 2005 Yahoo paid $1 billion for 40% of Alibaba. When Verizon bought the internet business, what remained of Yahoo Inc. was renamed Altaba in June 2017: an investment company holding the Alibaba and Yahoo Japan stakes. It sold them and dissolved in October 2019. For its last years, the company’s stock market value rested more on that 2005 investment than on Yahoo’s own products (analysis).

Is it finished? No, but it is smaller and private. Funds managed by Apollo Global Management bought 90% of Yahoo from Verizon in September 2021 (Verizon kept 10%). Yahoo left mainland China in November 2021 and cut about 1,000 of its 8,600 staff in 2023. It still runs Yahoo Mail, News, Finance, Sports and Search, and an advertising business.

Our view: the common thread is that Yahoo never settled what it was. It treated itself as a media portal while the web moved to search and then to social networks, and it bought and rented the core capability (search) instead of owning it. The breaches show the cost of treating security as overhead: $350 million came off the sale price. The other view: this reading leans on hindsight. In 1998 and 2002 nobody knew how valuable search advertising would become, the board could argue that in 2008 it was holding out for a fair price, and some argue that a portal company could not have turned into Google or Facebook whoever was in charge. Seen that way, Yahoo is less a lesson in mistakes than in how fast a market leader’s market can move.

What engineers can take (analysis): know the one capability your users choose you for, and keep it in your own hands; and treat security as part of what the business is worth, because it is priced when the business is sold.

eBay: split by function and by data, and no distributed transactions

What happened. Pierre Omidyar founded eBay as AuctionWeb in September 1995, as a hobby site; it took the name eBay in 1997. By 2008 its architect Randy Shoup described a site with over two billion page views a day, and the practices it had learned to scale it. eBay partitioned by function: selling, bidding and search ran on separate pools of application servers (“roughly 16,000 application servers into 220 different pools”), and there was “no single monolithic database”, but separate databases for users, items and purchases, “1000 logical databases in all, on 400 physical hosts”. It then split each one horizontally: user data, for example, was divided over 20 hosts, “if you can’t split it, you can’t scale it”. Because listing one new item could touch “twenty or thirty different hosts”, it allowed “absolutely no client-side or distributed transactions of any kind - no two-phase commit”, and reached eventual consistency instead through careful ordering of database operations, asynchronous recovery events and reconciliation batches. Its other practices were to decouple components asynchronously, move work onto asynchronous flows, put a layer of indirection in front of databases and search, and cache with care. InfoQ’s 2022 report on eBay recalls two of its vice-presidents, Shoup and Mark Weinberg, describing “systemic challenges with releasing major dependencies, leading to the equivalent of distributed monoliths”, and says eBay’s engineers built a system to release more than 3,000 dependent libraries in about two hours.

Our view: eBay is the older, pre-cloud version of the story many companies tell about microservices: split by business function, split again by key, and treat consistency as a choice per operation rather than all or nothing. The 2022 report shows the cost that follows: once the code is split, the dependencies between the parts become the thing to manage.

The other view: eBay’s scale was rare, and giving up transactions moves work into code that orders writes, retries and reconciles; most systems can keep ACID transactions in one database for a long time, and should until a measured limit says otherwise (see Distributed Systems and Database Design).

What you can take (analysis): decide per operation how much consistency it really needs; split data along the path it is read; prefer asynchronous hand-offs between parts that can wait. Don’t copy it if one database still handles your load: distributed data without distributed transactions is a lot of careful code to own.

Their own accounts: Randy Shoup, “Scalability Best Practices: Lessons from eBay” (InfoQ, May 2008); interview with Randy Shoup (InfoQ, June 2008, recorded at QCon San Francisco 2007); “eBay’s Architectural Principles” (talk, InfoQ, February 2008); and the 2022 report, “Managing Complex Dependencies with Distributed Architecture at eBay” (InfoQ, April 2022).

Amazon: internal services that became AWS

What happened. In the early 2000s Amazon pursued service-oriented architecture as a way to scale its engineering, and was frustrated with the speed of its software engineering. An engineering leader, Matt Round, recommended giving engineering teams as much autonomy as possible, adopting REST, standardising infrastructure, removing gate-keeping decision-makers, and continuous deployment. In July 2002 Amazon launched its first public web services, opening the Amazon.com platform to developers; over a hundred applications were built on it by 2004, which convinced Amazon that developers were “hungry for more”. Andy Jassy then mapped out an “Internet OS” of basic infrastructure building blocks. Amazon S3 storage launched on 14 March 2006, the first generally available AWS infrastructure service, followed by EC2 compute in August 2006.

Our view: AWS grew out of work to make Amazon’s own engineering faster, and outside developers’ demand for the 2002 web services confirmed there was a market for it. The lesson we draw is to build the platform your own teams need, and to treat outside demand as evidence.

The other view: few companies have Amazon’s scale, so the same decomposition into many independent services can cost a smaller organisation more in coordination than it saves; the conditions that made it pay off at Amazon may not hold elsewhere.

What you can take (analysis): standard, automated building blocks with clear interfaces let teams move without waiting on each other. Don’t copy it if you have a few teams: the coordination it removes may be smaller than the overhead it adds.

Their own account: Werner Vogels, “Modern applications at AWS” (All Things Distributed, August 2019)†.

Facebook (Meta): kept the PHP, changed the language and runtime underneath

What happened. Mark Zuckerberg launched “TheFacebook” on 4 February 2004, and facebook.com grew on a large PHP codebase. Rather than rewrite it in another language, Facebook changed what ran it. In February 2010 it announced HipHop for PHP, which translated PHP into C++ and compiled it; Wikipedia says its original motivation was to save resources on Facebook’s servers. It then built the HipHop Virtual Machine (HHVM), a just-in-time compiler, because HipHop had hit a plateau for performance and could not support all of PHP; HHVM matched HipHop’s performance on facebook.com in the last quarter of 2012, and Facebook deprecated HipHop in early 2013. In March 2014 Facebook released Hack, a PHP dialect with gradual static typing: “Over the last year, we have migrated nearly our entire PHP codebase to Hack, thanks to both organic adoption and a number of homegrown refactoring tools”, it wrote. Type checking was incremental, so a file could be partly converted. The post gives the reason for the work: at Facebook scale, “with thousands of engineers shipping new code twice a day”, errors that a dynamic language only shows at run time slowed everyone down. From HHVM 4.0, in February 2019, HHVM supported Hack only. Along the way Facebook also built and open-sourced tools that spread far beyond it, among them the Cassandra database (open-sourced in July 2008) and React (May 2013).

Our view: Facebook treated its language as something it could change, a little at a time, instead of something to escape with a rewrite: first a faster way to run the same code, then types that could be added file by file while the site kept shipping. It is the same idea as GitHub’s weekly upgrades, applied to the language itself.

The other view: building your own compiler, runtime and language needs a scale and a team that almost nobody has, and it leaves you maintaining a language few others use (HHVM dropped PHP support in 2019); in our view, for most teams, adding the type hints or type checker their own language already offers costs far less.

What you can take (analysis): when a codebase is too large to rewrite, look for changes that can be adopted gradually, with tools that do most of the work, while everything keeps running; types added file by file are one such change. Don’t copy it if your language or a standard tool already gives you that path, as Python’s type hints and TypeScript do for their languages.

Their own account: “Hack: a new programming language for HHVM” (Engineering at Meta, March 2014). The HipHop and HHVM history is from Wikipedia, which cites Facebook’s own posts.

Netflix: to the cloud after an outage, then failure on purpose

What happened. In August 2008 Netflix’s database was corrupted and the company could not ship DVDs to customers for three days, which led it to move all its data to the Amazon Web Services cloud; Netflix published “Completing the Netflix Cloud Migration” in February 2016, and by August 2016 it had closed its last physical data centre. During the migration, in 2011, Netflix built Chaos Monkey, a tool that randomly chooses a server in production and disables it during its usual hours of activity, to move from assuming nothing breaks to treating breakdowns as inevitable. It released the code in 2012, and Chaos Monkey became part of a wider “Simian Army”, which includes Chaos Kong, simulating the loss of a whole AWS region. Netflix’s Chaos Monkey repository describes the idea: “Exposing engineers to failures more frequently incentivizes them to build resilient services.”

Its circuit-breaker library Hystrix is now in maintenance mode. Netflix’s README says its focus moved to adaptive approaches that react to real-time performance, and that it uses resilience4j for new internal projects.

Our view: the outage showed that one database was a single point of failure; testing failure in production made resilience something checked every day rather than assumed. Netflix moving its own Hystrix to maintenance mode shows that a company’s tools can change while the idea behind them stays.

The other view: deliberately breaking production needs mature monitoring, automation and on-call practice first; without those, chaos experiments add risk, so many teams start in a test environment, or with game days, and grow from there (see Test Strategy and SRE).

What you can take (analysis): find your single points of failure before an outage does, and test failure regularly, starting small. Don’t copy it if you cannot yet see what your system is doing: monitoring and quick rollback come before deliberate failure.

Their own accounts: “Completing the Netflix Cloud Migration” (Netflix, February 2016)†; “The Netflix Simian Army” (Netflix Technology Blog, July 2011)†; Chaos Monkey and Hystrix on GitHub.

GitHub: one Rails monolith, kept and scaled

What happened. GitHub.com has been a Ruby on Rails monolith since the beginning. In 2023 GitHub wrote that it is nearly two million lines of code, that more than 1,000 engineers work on it daily, that it deploys as often as 20 times a day, and that nearly every week one of those deploys is a Rails upgrade. It was not always so: in 2018 the upgrade from Rails 3.2 to 5.2 took a year and a half, on a codebase then ten years old. Its data followed a similar path: GitHub.com was built around one main MySQL cluster, mysql1, and any incident there affected every feature stored on it. From 2019 GitHub grouped tables into “schema domains”, enforced the boundaries with SQL linters, and then moved them to separate clusters; by 2021 load on the hosts holding the former mysql1 data had fallen by 50%, with fewer database incidents.

Our view: GitHub kept the single application and drew boundaries inside it instead, first in the data, enforced by tools, before moving anything physically. Upgrading a little every week turned a year-and-a-half project into routine work.

The other view: a monolith this large needs heavy investment in tooling, testing and deployment to stay workable, and teams still share one codebase and one release train; organisations that want teams to choose their own languages and release on their own may still prefer separate services.

What you can take (analysis): draw boundaries inside the application first, and enforce them with tools; upgrade little and often instead of rarely and painfully. Don’t copy it if your teams truly need independent releases and different languages: then separate services may fit better.

Their own accounts: “Upgrading GitHub from Rails 3.2 to 5.2” (Eileen M. Uchitelle, September 2018); “Partitioning GitHub’s relational databases to handle scale” (Thomas Maurer, September 2021); “Building GitHub with Ruby and Rails” (Adam Hess, April 2023).

Shopify: built on Rails, scaled by splitting its data

What happened. Shopify was founded in 2006. According to Wikipedia, Tobias Lütke had built the online snowboard shop Snowdevil with Ruby on Rails, and the platform grew from it. Shopify created Liquid, an open-source template language written in Ruby, so shops could change their pages safely. As it grew, Shopify introduced sharding in 2014 to spread its data across multiple databases, and later moved to fully isolated instances. Shopify also publishes Packwerk, a Ruby gem “used to enforce boundaries and modularize Rails applications”: it groups files into packages, controls which constants a package makes public, and is meant to help existing codebases become more modular “without obstructing development”. On mobile, Shopify wrote in 2025 that over five years it had moved all its apps to React Native, so the same code could serve every platform.

Our view: when one database became the limit, Shopify split the data, first into shards and later into isolated instances, which changes how much one problem can affect. Packwerk shows a second idea it has published for Rails code in general: make the boundaries between parts of an application explicit and checked, before deciding whether to split it. (The sources we could read do not say how Shopify applies Packwerk to its own main application.)

The other view: sharding and isolated instances add their own operational work, such as moving data between shards and answering questions that span them; some teams prefer one larger database for as long as it lasts, to keep that work away.

What you can take (analysis): split data by a natural key (for Shopify, the shop) when one database is the limit; make module boundaries explicit and checked by a tool, as Packwerk does for Rails, before deciding whether to split the code. Don’t copy it if your data has no natural key that keeps most queries in one place: sharding then makes everyday queries harder.

Their own accounts: “E-Commerce at Scale: Inside Shopify’s Tech Stack” (Shopify Engineering)†; “Five years of React Native at Shopify” (Mustafa Ali, January 2025)†; Packwerk on GitHub.

Stack Overflow: its own data-access library, and a change in demand

What happened. Jeff Atwood and Joel Spolsky created Stack Overflow in 2008. It is written in C# with ASP.NET MVC, uses Microsoft SQL Server, and accesses data through Dapper, “a simple object mapper for .Net” that was originally developed for and by Stack Overflow, and which its team advertises as fast; Wikipedia cites Sam Saffron’s 2011 post “How I learned to stop worrying and write my own ORM” for it. In June 2021 Prosus agreed to buy the company for $1.8 billion. Then the questions fell: from over 100,000 a month in 2022 to a few thousand by early 2026, and by June 2026 more than 99% below the 2014 peak. Wikipedia attributes the fall to the arrival of AI coding tools and, according to some, a moderation culture seen as hostile. In the 2026 Stack Overflow Developer Survey, most respondents said they visit less often for simple questions, while a small group visit more to check answers from generative AI.

Our view: two lessons sit side by side. The engineering one is an inference: Stack Overflow built, and open-sourced, its own small data-access library instead of adopting a larger one, which we read as a choice to keep a stack its team could understand fully (Saffron’s post, which we could not open, gives their own reasons). The bigger one: the largest change in the company’s recent history came mainly from outside its architecture, from AI tools and, according to some, from a moderation culture, which is a community and product choice rather than an engineering one.

The other view: writing your own data-access layer is a cost few teams can justify, since well-supported general tools exist; and the decline may say less about engineering than about a product whose value, answering simple questions, moved to AI tools.

What you can take (analysis): watch how your users’ habits change, not only your system’s metrics; a healthy system can still lose its reason to exist. A small stack you understand end to end is easier to change when that happens. Don’t copy it if a well-supported general tool already meets your needs: writing and maintaining your own library then costs more than it saves.

Their own accounts: Sam Saffron, “How I learned to stop worrying and write my own ORM” (March 2011)†; Dapper on GitHub; Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2026.
