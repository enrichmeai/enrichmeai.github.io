Worried about AI?

Many people are, and many more do not yet know what is changing. A calm way to prepare: use what AI gives us, and keep the skills that let a person step in when it fails.

The tills are a good picture

Supermarkets once had a long row of tills, each with a person behind it. Over the years most have moved to a few staffed tills and a bank of self-service ones. Fewer people do the scanning. But the shop still needs people: someone approves an age check, clears a jammed scanner, helps the customer who is stuck, and knows what to do when the system goes down.

Software is going the same way. Fewer people will type routine code by hand, but people are still needed to manage the work, and managing it well takes a good understanding of what is being built. The people who stay most useful are the ones who understand what the machines are doing, and can take over when they stop.

We have been here before: CAD and CAM

Engineering design went through this once already. Drawings used to be made by hand at drawing boards, by teams of draughtsmen. Then computers took over the drawing and the machining:

  • 1940s and 1950s: the first numerically controlled machine tools follow points fed into them, instead of a person guiding every cut.
  • 1963: Ivan Sutherland writes Sketchpad for his PhD thesis, an early program for drawing on a computer screen.
  • 1982: AutoCAD is released for desktop computers, and computer-aided design (CAD) spreads to ordinary offices.
  • Computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) then turns those designs into instructions for the machines that cut and shape the parts.

The drawing board disappeared, and many drafting jobs changed or went. But engineering did not end: it grew. The people who did well learned the new tools and kept the judgement the tools could not supply: whether a part would be strong enough, whether it could be made, whether it was safe. CAD made drawing faster; it never decided what was worth drawing.

AI is the same kind of shift for writing code and documents, faster and wider. The lesson carries over: learn the tool, keep the understanding, and own the decisions.

Checked against (Wikipedia): Computer numerical control; Sketchpad; AutoCAD; Computer-aided design; Computer-aided manufacturing.

The risk of a team that can only prompt

Picture a team whose only skill is asking an AI to do the work. Everything is automated. Then something fails, and the only thing they know how to do is ask the AI to fix it. If the AI is wrong, unavailable, or part of what failed, nobody in the room can help.

We have seen what large automated failures look like. On 19 July 2024 a faulty update to a widely used security product crashed about 8.5 million Windows computers, by Microsoft's estimate, and many had to be repaired by hand, one machine at a time. People with hands-on skills were the way out.

Keep a hand on the controls

The Boeing 737 MAX accidents of October 2018 and March 2019 carry a lesson worth remembering. Official investigations found that a new automated flight-control feature, MCAS, contributed to both crashes, and references to it had been removed from the pilots' flight manual. When it misbehaved, the crews did not have what they needed to take back control.

Agents that heal themselves are useful. But every automated system needs a documented manual override, and people who have practised using it. In an emergency, a person should be able to step in and fix things directly, not only through another agent.

We will use it more than we meant to

Nobody knows for sure where this goes. But rightly or wrongly, most of us will end up using AI more than we intended. That can be good or bad.

It is good for someone who has the idea and knows the specification but could never find the hundreds of hours to build it. For people with that experience, it is a genuinely useful tool, and solo builders who want to make a product should start: doing something beats waiting. It is bad when it becomes a habit of prompting and never going back to check. A mind that stops checking slowly stops being able to.

So use the time it gives back well. If AI saves you hours, spend some of them reading the foundation books you never had time for.

More with fewer, or more with the same

Come back to the bulldozer. Companies may expect the same work from fewer engineers, or more work from the same number. We can already see some reduction across the industry, and there will be disruption, though no one can say yet how much.

The people who come through it best will be the ones who can direct the machine and still understand the ground it is moving.

Seven ways people use AI

  • To automate without learning. Fast today, fragile tomorrow: when it breaks, nothing was learned.
  • To learn. Ask it to explain, then check the explanation against the docs.
  • As a tester and reviewer. Ask it to find the cases you missed, try to break your code, and review your writing, designs and pull requests.
  • As a tutor and mentor. Ask it to quiz you, review your reasoning, and say what to read next.
  • To explore and research. Summarise a long document, compare options, brainstorm, draft something new; then check what comes back against the real sources.
  • As a builder. Use it to build what you design and understand: frameworks, tools, products, reusable blueprints for your team. You set the architecture and the standards; AI speeds up the making. See Frameworks vs AI.
  • To automate, with oversight. Let it, or an agent, run routine work you understand. Watch what it does, keep the logs, and keep the off switch. See "Keep a hand on the controls" above and Agent Harness.

Only the first makes you weaker; the rest make you stronger, as long as you stay the one who understands.

You may not read every line, but you must understand it and test it

With AI writing more of the code, we may not read every line it produces. That can be acceptable, on two conditions: we understand most of it, how it is shaped, what it depends on, and where it can fail; and we can test all of it in the right way, even the lines we did not read. Tests, types, reviews and monitoring become how we know the code works. See Testing and Observability.

Every role is changing, not only engineering

In a large company the change reaches everyone: business analysts, journey managers, product owners, software engineers, solution architects, enterprise architects, data engineers, data analysts, testers, and engineers who have spent careers on mainframes and are now learning cloud and AI. None of us is finished learning, whatever the job title says. Many people are worried simply because they do not yet know what is changing. There will be work, in some capacity, for people who are ready to use AI in the right way.

What helps most is not one course or one tool. It is the will to adapt: understanding what is changing, trying it, and changing how we work. It will be hard, and some days it will feel slow. Learning one small thing every day still adds up: a command, a concept, a page of this guide, a question asked out loud.

  • Analysts, journey managers and product owners: learn enough of how AI works to write requirements it can meet, and to spot where it should not be trusted. Your knowledge of customers and rules is what checks its answers.
  • Software engineers: AI can write more of the code, so the job moves towards design, review, testing and running it in production. Keep the foundations sharp enough to judge what it produced. See Testing and Claude Code & Cowork.
  • Solution architects: AI becomes another component to design around: where it sits, what data it may see, how it fails, what it costs, and where a person must stay in the loop. See Architecture and Agent Harness.
  • Enterprise architects: set the guard rails across the organisation: which models and tools are approved, how data is governed, how agents are given access, and how the estate changes without a big-bang rewrite. See Frameworks vs AI.
  • Data engineers and analysts: AI is only as good as the data it reads. Quality, lineage and access control matter more, not less. See Data Engineering.
  • Engineers coming from mainframes and older platforms: what you know about reliability, batch, transactions and running systems for decades is exactly what new platforms often lack. Bring it with you; learn the new tools on top of it.

Whatever your role

  • Learn what it is and is not. AI predicts likely text; it can be fluent and wrong. AI for Engineers and LLMs are written plainly.
  • Use it on your own work, and check it. You learn its strengths and its mistakes fastest on things you already know well.
  • Keep your domain knowledge. Knowing your customers, your trade and your rules is what tells you when an answer is wrong.
  • Invest in people skills. Explaining, listening and deciding together are hard to automate. See Being a Good Human.

If you are an engineer

  • Be able to fix it without the agent. Read the logs, follow a request through the system, roll back a release. Linux, Networking and Observability are where that starts.
  • Write runbooks a person can follow at 3 a.m., and keep them next to the automation they cover. See SRE.
  • Build the off switch. Every agent gets a kill switch, a manual fallback and limits on what it may touch. See Agent Harness and Agents on GCP.
  • Practise the bad day. Rehearse an outage with the automation turned off, so taking over is a habit, not a hope.

Keep your own memory

As AI does more of the work, what you have learned (how your systems behave, why decisions were made, what worked and what did not) becomes more valuable, not less. Keep it in a form you own, so it stays useful to you over the years and goes with you from job to job.

That is what we are building with Headworks, Penstock and Cistern running together: a self-hosted AI agent that runs on your own machine with a model you pick. Its memory is plain files you own and can read, change or delete, and it reads only the data you choose to share, with a receipt for every read. What you build up stays yours, and you decide what to share with others. See Our open source.

A journey, not a fight

When change feels threatening, the body reacts as if to danger: the fight-or-flight response. It helps in a real emergency and hurts in a long change, because a mind in that state reacts instead of thinking. The change AI brings will take years, not days, so treat it as a journey rather than a fight. It is a journey, and we are in competition with no one: not our colleagues, not the machines.

  • Step back from the headlines. Ask what is actually changing in your own work this month, not what the loudest voice predicts.
  • Learn something new every day. One thing learned each day compounds; Atomic Habits calls it getting about 1% better each day. See the Reading list.
  • Look after the basics. Sleep, movement and time away from screens keep you calm enough to think. See Body, Mind & Nature.
  • Keep doing the work. Good things happen to people who are ready and keep going, day after day, long after the headlines move on.
  • Think above the ordinary. When everyone is anxious, the person who stays calm, learns steadily and helps others becomes the one people turn to.

Understanding before opinion

Every day brings millions of posts about AI, and everyone has an opinion. We have come to find more satisfaction in expressing a view than in understanding the thing itself, and we miss the experience of learning it.

That is why this guide is a quiet, static site: no feed, no comments, no tracking. Come, find a section, read it in peace, try the code, and save it as a PDF if you like. Understanding first; the opinion can wait.

Believe in yourself

"When you are inspired by some great purpose, some extraordinary project, all your thoughts break their bonds: Your mind transcends limitations, your consciousness expands in every direction, and you find yourself in a new, great and wonderful world. Dormant forces, faculties and talents become alive, and you discover yourself to be a greater person by far than you ever dreamed yourself to be."
A passage often attributed to Patanjali

Whoever wrote it, the idea is true: the limits we feel are often limits in how we see ourselves. In times of change, belief in yourself is what lets you start learning something new instead of waiting to be told you can.

  • Belief is built, not found. The psychologist Albert Bandura called it self-efficacy: your belief that you can succeed at a particular task. He found the most effective way to build it is mastery experiences, small successes of your own.
  • So start small and finish. Run one command, fix one bug, read one chapter. Each thing you complete is evidence you can do the next.
  • Watch your self-image. "I am not technical" or "I am too old to learn this" become true only if you act on them. Psycho-Cybernetics and As a Man Thinketh on the Reading list are about exactly this.
  • Find a purpose bigger than the worry. As the passage says, a purpose you care about wakes up abilities you did not know you had.

Checked against: Self-efficacy (Wikipedia).

Harness it, and keep control

Like every technology before it, AI is worth using for what it gives us, and worth controlling for what it can break. Use it every day. Keep the foundations, keep the manual override, and keep people who know how to use both.
