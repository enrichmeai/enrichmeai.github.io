Guide / Architecture & design / Databricks

Databricks: from a Berkeley lab to a $190 billion company

Seven researchers who built Apache Spark started a company to sell it. Thirteen years later it is valued at $190 billion, and it is still private. The story, what it actually sells, how its valuation is worked out, and whether it could become one of the biggest software listings ever.

The short answer

Databricks sells a platform where companies store, process and analyse their data, and increasingly build AI on top of it. It grew out of Apache Spark, a tool for crunching very large amounts of data across many computers, which its founders created at the University of California, Berkeley. In August 2026 it closed a funding round at a $190 billion valuation, on a revenue run rate of about $7 billion a year, and it is still private.

For engineers it is a good case study twice over: an open-source project that became a business, and a live example of how investors put a price on a fast-growing company (see How Companies Are Valued).

From Spark to a company

  • 2009: Matei Zaharia starts Spark at Berkeley’s AMPLab. It is open-sourced in 2010. For repeated calculations, such as training machine-learning models, it could cut the time taken by several orders of magnitude compared with Hadoop MapReduce, the main big-data tool of the day.
  • 2013: Spark is donated to the Apache Software Foundation, and seven of its creators, Ali Ghodsi, Andy Konwinski, Arsalan Tavakoli-Shiraji, Ion Stoica, Matei Zaharia, Patrick Wendell and Reynold Xin, found Databricks to commercialise it. Andreessen Horowitz leads a $14 million first round.
  • 2014–2017: Spark becomes a top-level Apache project; Databricks launches its first cloud product; Microsoft offers it inside Azure as Azure Databricks.
  • 2023: Databricks buys the generative AI start-up MosaicML, reported at $1.3 billion, and launches its Data Intelligence Platform, joining its data platform with generative AI.
  • 2024: It open-sources Unity Catalog, its tool for governing who can see and use which data.

Ion Stoica was the first chief executive; Ali Ghodsi took over in 2016.

What it actually sells: the lakehouse

Companies have traditionally kept data in two kinds of places: a data warehouse, tidy and fast for business reports, and a data lake, cheap storage for everything else, including raw files, images and logs. Databricks popularised the lakehouse, which combines the two. Its open-source Delta Lake project adds reliable database-style transactions to files in a data lake.

One design choice matters a lot to customers: the data stays in the customer’s own cloud storage, in open formats such as Delta Lake and Apache Iceberg, while Databricks provides the computing on top. Industry analysts note that this separation of storage and compute reduces lock-in. Around that core it now sells tools for machine learning, vector search for retrieval (see RAG), serving models, and building AI agents.

Analysis: this is the argument behind the valuation: Databricks sells the layer where a company’s data already lives. If AI for a company is built on its own data, the platform that holds the data is well placed to sell the AI too.

How a $190 billion valuation is worked out

Databricks has no share price, because it is not listed. Its valuation is the price per share agreed in each funding round, multiplied by all its shares. The latest round, led by Coatue in July 2026 and closed in August at $5 billion, set the company at $190 billion after the new money (“post-money”). Blackstone, MGX, T. Rowe Price and Sixth Street Growth also invested.

For a fast-growing software company, investors usually reason from revenue, because profits are small or still to come. A quick way to see the price:

StepNumber
Valuation after the August 2026 round$190 billion
Revenue run rate at the time$7 billion a year
Valuation divided by run rateabout 27 times revenue
Growth reported in June 202680% a year, on a $6.9 billion run rate

A run rate takes recent revenue, often one month or quarter, and scales it up to a year. It flatters a company that is growing fast, which is exactly why investors use it: they are paying for where revenue is heading. Databricks also reported positive free cash flow over the twelve months before February 2026, which supports the price. The full toolkit, from multiples to discounted cash flow, is on How Companies Are Valued.

Will it be the biggest software IPO ever?

“Biggest” can mean two things: the company’s value when it lists, or the money raised in the listing. For comparison, Snowflake, a data-warehouse rival, raised $3.4 billion when it listed in September 2020, one of the largest software IPOs.

Our view: if Databricks lists anywhere near its last private price, it would be one of the largest software listings there has been, and its position under enterprise AI is the reason investors keep paying more for it.

The other view: private valuations are set by a small group of investors and can fall when a company meets the public market; a price of about 27 times revenue assumes very fast growth continues for years. Competition is fierce, from Snowflake and from the cloud providers’ own data services, and AI spending may not keep rising at today’s pace. And Databricks may simply choose to stay private, as it has so far.

Reports of new funding talks at higher valuations appear often. We include only rounds that have closed and been reported, and update this page when a new one closes.

When Databricks fits, by requirement

If this is true for youIt leans towards
You already run Spark, or have large data-engineering and machine-learning workloadsDatabricks, which grew out of Spark
You want your data to stay in your own cloud storage in open formatsA lakehouse with Delta Lake or Iceberg, Databricks or another engine
Your work is mostly SQL reporting on structured data, with a small teamA managed warehouse may be simpler (see BigQuery)
You are all-in on one cloud and want fewer vendorsThat cloud’s own data services; Azure also offers Azure Databricks
Your team already uses a data platformStay with it unless a requirement above forces a move: migration costs time and money

What engineers can take from it

  • Open source can be the start of a business, not the opposite of one: Spark built the audience Databricks sells to.
  • Academic research can become infrastructure the whole industry runs on.
  • Owning the place where data lives is a strong position when AI arrives.
