A brief history of software systems

How we got here, in plain words: each era solved the last one's pain and rediscovered the same hard problems. Knowing the story makes today's tools easier to understand, and easier to judge.

The thread through it all

Every era wrestled with the same four questions: how do two programs agree on a contract, where does state live, what happens when something fails, and how do we see what went wrong? The answers moved from heavy and agreed in advance, to light and flexible, and back again whenever the light way hurt. The questions never went away.

Why learning how it evolved matters

Our view, with the ideas it rests on, and the other view at the end. Tools change every few years; the problems they solve change far more slowly. Knowing how we got here is how you tell a new idea from an old one with a new name, and how you choose and fix things without guessing.

  • You recognise the pattern when it comes back. The same four questions run through every era on this page (contracts, state, failure, seeing what went wrong), and the answers swing between “agreed in advance” and “light and flexible”. When a new tool arrives, you can ask which swing it is, and what the last one learned.
  • You know why the fence is there. Chesterton’s fence (see Philosophy): do not remove something until you know why it was put up. Most “legacy” code is a decision whose reason was forgotten; history is how you get the reason back.
  • You avoid known traps. Fred Brooks named the second-system effect in The Mythical Man-Month (1975), from IBM’s move to the far more ambitious OS/360: after a small, clean first system, the second tends to become over-engineered, because the designer adds every idea deferred the first time. Rewrites still fall into it. As George Santayana put it: “Those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it.”
  • You judge what will last. The Lindy effect is a heuristic, not a law: for things that do not wear out, such as ideas and technologies, the longer something has already lasted, the longer it is likely to keep lasting. It is a reason to learn the long-lived foundations on this guide’s Foundations pages first, and to treat this year’s framework as unproven until it has survived a few years.
  • You choose by requirement, not fashion. Knowing what problem a tool was built for (see Apps, Build Tools, UI History) tells you whether that problem is yours.
  • You can work with AI on old systems. Models produce code in the style of every era they learned from, and many systems people rely on are decades old. Knowing the history helps you recognise an outdated pattern in a suggestion, and maintain the old system with care rather than fear.

New engineers are lucky (our view). What experienced engineers learned over 25 to 30 years, one release, outage and rewrite at a time, is now written down, explained and searchable, and AI can walk you through it. If you are ready to put in the work, you can learn in two or three years what took them decades to live through. The history on this page is that shortcut.

The other view: history can turn into nostalgia (“we did this in 2005”) and into a reason to dismiss things that really are better. Tools do improve. Learn the why behind each change, not the trivia, and never use the past only to say no. And reading is not the same as living through it: judgement from your own incidents and mistakes still takes time, so build things and run them while you read.

A look back: technology goes in cycles

The same ideas keep coming back, each time on cheaper hardware, faster networks and better tools. Knowing the earlier turn of the wheel tells you what the new one will get wrong.

Analysis, and the other view: it is less a circle than a spiral. Each return happens because a constraint changed (hardware got cheap, networks got fast, operations got automated), and it comes back with lessons from the last time. The skill is to ask, of any “new” idea: what changed that makes it work now, when it struggled before?

Microservices and CORBA: not the same, but alike in places

Our view, built on the facts below, with the other view at the end. Microservices are not the new CORBA, but they share some of its problems, and a badly split system can repeat its mistakes. How alike they are depends on how the services were split.

  • The similarities. Both spread one system across a network, so both pay the same tax. Peter Deutsch’s list of the fallacies of distributed computing (Sun, 1994), which begins “the network is reliable” and “latency is zero”, applies to both. Fine-grained synchronous calls between services that share a database and release together recreate distributed objects: a distributed monolith.
  • The differences. CORBA’s location transparency, treating a remote object like a local one, is the part most criticised. The 2014 article by James Lewis and Martin Fowler that named microservices does the opposite. It defines them as independently deployable services organised around business capabilities, and says remote calls are more expensive, so remote APIs need to be coarser-grained. Done that way, it is an organisational choice, not a remote-object technology.
  • Are we only surviving because of modern tools? Partly. Container platforms, service meshes, retries, circuit breakers and distributed tracing absorb pain that earlier teams carried by hand. But the same tools make failure visible and recovery automatic, which is an improvement, not just a mask.
  • A one-question test (our view): can one team deploy one service on a Tuesday afternoon without coordinating with anyone else? If not, the system has the costs of distribution without the benefit, and the tools are keeping it alive.

The other view: many teams would do better to start with a modular monolith and split only when team size or scaling demands it (see Microservices). Others point out that at large scale, with many teams, independent deployment is the only thing that keeps them moving at all. Both are true for different requirements.

Distributed objects: everything agreed in advance

  • CORBA, from the Object Management Group, let programs in different languages call each other's objects across a network. You wrote the contract in an interface definition language and generated client stubs and server skeletons before anything could talk.
  • Java RMI did the same between Java programs, more simply, and Java could load the classes it needed over the network.
  • Application servers such as WebLogic wrapped this with transactions, security and pooling. Powerful, and heavy.
  • What it taught us: a remote call is not a local call. It can be slow, fail halfway, or never answer.

Enterprise JavaBeans: the beans we lived with

  • Session beans held business logic. Stateless ones served any caller and were pooled, so they suited reads and simple operations. Stateful ones kept a conversation with one client across calls.
  • Stateful session beans and database locks were how some booking and ticketing systems held a seat while a customer finished: the bean kept the conversation, the database kept the lock.
  • Entity beans represented rows in the database, with the container managing how they were loaded and saved. They were heavy and slow to work with, and plain Java objects mapped by tools such as Hibernate, and later JPA, replaced them.
  • Message-driven beans consumed messages from a JMS queue or topic, bringing event-driven work inside the application server.
  • What it taught us: the ideas (stateless services, conversations, transactions, messaging) were right; the weight was not. Spring, and later EJB itself, kept the ideas and dropped the ceremony.

Models, UML and generated screens

  • UML, which appeared in 1997, gave teams one language for drawing systems: class diagrams, sequence diagrams, use cases. Grady Booch, James Rumbaugh and Ivar Jacobson brought their methods together into it.
  • Model-driven architecture, from the Object Management Group, went further: draw the model, then generate the code from it. It worked well for some layers and poorly for the rest.
  • XSLT transformed XML into HTML, so screens could be generated from data and templates rather than written by hand.
  • What it taught us: generating code from a model is powerful when the model is the truth, and painful when people edit the generated code. The same lesson applies to AI-generated code today.

The early web: stateless by design

  • HTTP forgets you between requests. To remember who you were, pages carried state in hidden form fields, in the URL (URL rewriting) and, increasingly, in cookies.
  • HTML was standardised in the mid-1990s, but browsers still differed: code checked whether it ran in Internet Explorer or Netscape, and what we now call Ajax worked first through Microsoft's XMLHTTP object.
  • Caching mattered because bandwidth was precious on dial-up: browsers and proxies kept copies, and pages said how long a copy stayed fresh.
  • What it taught us: statelessness scales, and every bit of state has to live somewhere on purpose.

Pages made on the server

  • CGI scripts started a new process for every request.
  • PHP began in 1994 as Rasmus Lerdorf's "Personal Home Page" tools and grew into a language for building web pages on the server.
  • Java servlets and JSP ran inside a long-lived server, handling many requests without starting a process each time.
  • Struts, an Apache project founded in May 2000, brought the model-view-controller pattern to Java web applications.

Runtimes get fast, and get company

  • Java began by interpreting bytecode, which was slow. Just-in-time (JIT) compilers, which compile the busiest code to machine code while the program runs, made it fast, and the JVM's HotSpot became the standard approach.
  • Microsoft's C# and .NET arrived in the early 2000s with many of the same ideas: a managed runtime, garbage collection and code compiled just in time.
  • What it taught us: a good runtime lets you write simpler code and still go fast, and ideas spread quickly once they work.

Integration: buses, services and lighter ways

  • SOAP web services brought back contracts agreed in advance, written in WSDL, with generated clients, much as CORBA had done.
  • Enterprise service buses put routing, transformation and orchestration into one large central product.
  • Lighter tools followed. Apache Camel implemented the patterns from Hohpe and Woolf's Enterprise Integration Patterns as a library. Spring came into being in 2003, by its own account, as a response to the complexity of the early J2EE specifications.
  • What it taught us: one big central hub becomes the bottleneck and the single point of failure.

REST, long journeys, and sagas

  • REST, which Roy Fielding described in his doctoral dissertation, used the web's own design (resources, URLs, standard methods, caching) instead of a custom remote-call layer.
  • Without distributed transactions, a long piece of work becomes a journey of steps. If it fails halfway, you undo the completed steps in reverse order, which only works if you recorded every step you took.
  • This idea is old. It was described as the "saga" in a 1987 database paper by Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem, long before microservices made the word popular again. See Architecture.

One runnable JAR: from app servers to "java -jar"

For years a Java web application was a WAR file deployed into a separate application server. Then frameworks started shipping the server inside the application: one file you start with java -jar.

  • Dropwizard (first published to Maven Central in December 2011) bundled an embedded Jetty server with Jersey, Jackson, Logback and Metrics, and its getting-started guide builds the whole application as a "fat JAR". As I remember it, that was the first time many of us ran a service this way, before Spring Boot (1.0 in April 2014) made the idea mainstream.
  • How the fat JAR is built: the Maven Shade Plugin unpacks every dependency into one archive. Its ServicesResourceTransformer merges the META-INF/services files so libraries such as Jersey and Jackson do not overwrite each other's, and its ManifestResourceTransformer sets the Main-Class, so java -jar app.jar server config.yml just works.
  • Why it mattered: one file to build, copy and run made services simple to deploy, and fitted containers perfectly a few years later. See Docker and Spring Boot.

Checked against: Maven Central: dropwizard-core, first release dated 22 December 2011; Maven Central: spring-boot, first release dated 1 April 2014; Dropwizard README and getting-started guide; the transformer classes inside the maven-shade-plugin artefact.

Why Spring lasted, and how it helped Java

Rod Johnson released the first version of Spring with his book Expert One-on-One J2EE Design and Development in October 2002, as a lighter way to build enterprise Java than Enterprise JavaBeans; it was released under the Apache licence in June 2003. Many of the tools in the table below faded. Spring did not, and the reasons are worth learning:

  • It solved the real pain. Plain Java objects, dependency injection, and transactions set up by configuration rather than by the EJB container. Ordinary code became easy to write and to test.
  • It kept changing with the platform. Annotations instead of XML, Spring Boot's runnable JAR and sensible defaults (1.0 in April 2014), then a sixth major version of the framework (November 2022) that moved to a Java 17 baseline and the jakarta namespace. It adopted new Java features rather than fighting them. See Spring Boot.
  • It integrated rather than replaced. It worked with the libraries people already used (Hibernate, messaging, web frameworks) instead of asking them to throw everything away.
  • It stayed open and backward-compatible enough that a team could upgrade step by step over the years, without rewrites.

How that helped Java: when enterprise Java was heavy, Spring made it productive again, and when cloud and containers arrived, Spring Boot made Java a natural fit for small runnable services. Keeping millions of developers productive on the JVM gave Java time to modernise itself: faster releases, records, virtual threads. (The reasons above are this guide's analysis; the dates are sourced.)

Checked against (Wikipedia): Spring Framework; Spring Boot.

Microservices, cloud, and seeing what happens

  • Microservices split systems into small services that each own their data. They brought back every distributed-systems problem at once, now spread across many teams.
  • For years, failures were hard to follow from one service to the next. Observability caught up: logs, metrics and traces, and a shared trace context passed with every call (the W3C traceparent header). See Observability.
  • The cloud turned servers into something you rent by the minute and declare in code.

The people behind the ideas

We remember the famous product launches and forget many of the people whose ideas we use every day. A few worth knowing, and reading:

  • The "Gang of Four", Erich Gamma, Richard Helm, Ralph Johnson and John Vlissides, wrote Design Patterns: Elements of Reusable Object-Oriented Software, which gave us a shared vocabulary: factory, observer, strategy, adapter.
  • Martin Fowler wrote Refactoring (its second edition with Kent Beck) and Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture, and has explained ideas such as continuous integration and microservices to a generation of engineers. He calls the Gang of Four's book "the best book ever written on object-oriented design".
  • Kent Beck developed Extreme Programming, and test-driven development within it, in the late 1990s.
  • Grady Booch, James Rumbaugh and Ivar Jacobson created UML. Gregor Hohpe and Bobby Woolf wrote Enterprise Integration Patterns. Roy Fielding described REST. Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem described sagas.
  • And thousands of open-source maintainers whose names most of us never learn, whose work runs in almost everything we build.

Before you ask an AI to explain a pattern, read the person who named it. The original is usually clearer, and it tells you why. Start with Fowler on the Gang of Four, Extreme Programming and test-driven development.

And now, AI

AI can generate the code that each of these eras made us write by hand: the stubs, the boilerplate, the glue. But the four questions have not changed: contracts, state, failure, and seeing what went wrong. People who know this history recognise an old problem in a new form, and do not mistake generated code for understanding. See AI for Engineers.
