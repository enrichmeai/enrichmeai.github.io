Well Rounded Engineer

More human

A field guide for engineers in the AI era, and for anyone who wants to do good work and live well. AI hands everyone a bulldozer; this is about being the site manager: clear thinking and good character first, fundamentals before shortcuts, then what to know across data, systems, design, cloud, delivery and AI, what each level of experience looks like, and where to learn more by doing.

The well-rounded engineer

Well Rounded Engineer — More Human is a field guide for engineers in the AI era: people who think clearly, behave well, master the fundamentals, know enough of everything to work across the whole system, go deep where it matters, and use AI as one more engineering tool. Use this page to see how much of each tab you need.

What well-rounded means here

  • The base: character and thinking. Honesty, curiosity, care for people, and clear reasoning grounded in mathematics, the scientific method and philosophy. AI can generate code; it cannot supply judgement or integrity. See Behaviours and the Thinking & character tabs.
  • The foundation: shared fundamentals. Every engineer needs these: SQL, one language well (Java or Python), Linux, Docker, networking and the web, Git and CI/CD, testing, security basics, and reading logs.
  • Breadth across the system. Enough of data, services, design, cloud and operations to read the code, follow a design discussion, and find the right person or doc.
  • Depth where you choose. Real expertise in one or two areas. Many engineers on this kind of system go deep in data (BigQuery, dbt, pipelines) or platform (cloud, Kubernetes, delivery), or both; the shape is sometimes drawn as a T or a Y.
  • AI across all of it. You build features on models and use assistants to work faster, with the same discipline as everything else.

"Know enough" below is the bar for breadth: what any engineer should manage without help. "Go deep" is what a specialist in that area owns.

Not only for engineers

We get one life, and whatever job we do, we can make the journey beautiful. The tabs under Thinking & character (Mathematics, Science & Method, Philosophy, Being a Good Human and Body, Mind & Nature) and the Behaviours page are written for engineers, but they hold for anyone: a nurse, a teacher, an accountant, a builder. Read them that way. The engineering tabs show what "fundamentals first" looks like in one craft; every craft has its own.

Fundamentals first: be the site manager, not a vibe coder

AI hands every engineer a bulldozer. A bulldozer on a building site is only safe with a site manager who knows how buildings stand up: the foundations, the materials, the regulations, and what to inspect before anyone moves in. "Vibe coding", accepting whatever the model produces because it looks right, is a bulldozer with nobody in charge of the site. It feels fast until the first thing breaks and nobody can say why.

How to learn this way:

  • Do it by hand once. Write the loop, the SQL query, the Dockerfile, the deploy, the test yourself before asking AI to do it for you. Then you can judge its version.
  • Use AI as a tutor while learning: ask it to explain, to quiz you, to review your attempt. Do not let it write the answer you are trying to learn.
  • Earn the bulldozer: the more fundamentals you own, the more work you can safely hand to AI, because you can inspect it.
  • Follow the order: Foundations, then a language, then quality and security, then the area you want to go deep in. The Home Lab turns each into practice.
  • Keep going back to basics: when something surprises you, the gap is almost always in a fundamental, not in the tool.

AI raises the floor of what anyone can produce. Fundamentals raise the ceiling of what you can be trusted with.

An engineer who uses AI, not a prompt engineer

Writing a good prompt is one skill among many, and the smallest one. What makes AI work in production is engineering:

  • Data: the model is only as good as what you retrieve and feed it. That is SQL, pipelines, chunking and freshness, the data tabs of this guide.
  • Evaluation: a test set and a score, so a change to the prompt or model is measured, not judged by eye.
  • Systems: timeouts, retries, caching, cost limits, logging, and a fallback when the model is down or refuses.
  • Security: untrusted text can carry instructions (prompt injection). Least-privilege tools, no secrets in prompts, output treated as untrusted input.
  • Judgement with assistants: AI-written code gets the same review, tests and doc checks as yours. You stay responsible for what ships.

The AI tab covers each of these. A prompt engineer stops at the prompt; a well-rounded engineer ships, measures and operates the whole feature.

The four levels used on every tab

1 Foundation

Can use it with examples and guidance. Reads it, runs it, makes small changes safely, and knows when to ask.

2 Practitioner

Works independently. Builds and ships features with it, tests them, debugs ordinary problems, follows the team's standards.

3 Senior

Designs with it. Makes trade-offs, reviews others' work, handles the hard incidents, and mentors.

4 Expert

Sets direction across teams. Knows the internals, writes the standards and blueprints, and leads migrations and decisions.

Where a well-rounded engineer aims: Senior in the one or two areas they go deep in; Practitioner in the foundations, testing, security, and the thinking and character tabs; at least Foundation everywhere else. Expert in one or two areas comes with time and is not expected of everyone.

Each tab ends with its own level expectations and an "Explore further" card of official docs, books and topics to study next.

Self-check: the foundations

You should be able to do all of these without asking:

  • Explain what a query will cost before you run it.
  • Read a stack trace or a job log and name the line that failed.
  • Say why a service cannot reach another: DNS, firewall, or auth.
  • Read a Terraform plan and spot a destroy or a replace.
  • Write a test that fails before your fix and passes after it.
  • Roll back a bad deploy.
  • Get a shell in a running container and find why it exited.
  • Say what a prompt-based feature returns when the model is down, and how you would know.

Self-check: AI

  • Return validated JSON from a model instead of parsing free text.
  • Build a small eval set and show a change improved the score.
  • Explain how retrieved documents could smuggle instructions into a prompt, and how you limit the damage.
  • Estimate the monthly cost of a feature from tokens per call and calls per day.
  • Reject an AI-suggested change because a test or the docs show it is wrong.

Self-check: depth in data

  • Design a partitioned, clustered table for a given query pattern.
  • Choose between merge, insert_overwrite and microbatch for a dbt model, and explain why.
  • Make a DAG rerun for one date without duplicating data.
  • Find rows a parse silently turned into NULL, and stop it happening again.
  • Explain a BigQuery bill from INFORMATION_SCHEMA.JOBS.

Self-check: depth in platform

  • Write the Terraform for a Cloud Run job, its service account, its IAM and its schedule.
  • Give a service a fixed egress IP and private access to Cloud SQL.
  • Ship a canary revision and move traffic in steps.
  • Size memory and CPU for a JVM container and justify the numbers.
  • Debug a CrashLoopBackOff or a "failed to listen on PORT" from logs alone.

Suggested order for a newcomer

  1. Thinking & character: Mathematics, Science & Method, Philosophy, Being a Good Human, Body, Mind & Nature
  2. Foundations: Linux, Networking, Web Fundamentals, Docker, SQL Skills, Database Design
  3. Languages: Java, Python
  4. Quality & security: Testing, Security, OAuth & OIDC
  5. Data: Data Engineering, Data Libraries, Culvert, dbt, Airflow DAGs
  6. Systems: APIs & Integration, GraphQL, Kafka, Payment Gateways, Spring Boot, Quarkus
  7. Design: Architecture, Microservices, Event Sourcing, System Design, Performance, Caching
  8. Cloud: GCP vs AWS vs Azure, Data on GCP, BigQuery, Cloud Run, Apigee
  9. Delivery & ops: DevOps, Observability, Ops Tooling, SRE, Harness, Terraform, Kubernetes, Helm Charts, Operators, Service Mesh & Istio
  10. AI: LLMs, AI for Engineers, Frameworks vs AI, Claude Code & Cowork, GitHub Copilot, Agent Harness, RAG, MCP
  11. Decentralised web: Web 3.0, Solid

Then pick the area you want to go deep in and work through its self-check until every item is a yes.
