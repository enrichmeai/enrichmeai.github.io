Guide / Architecture & design / Palantir

Palantir: how it makes money

A software company that spent twenty years losing money, built its business on spy agencies and armies, then became one of the most valuable companies in the world. What it sells, who pays, how its unusual sales model works, why investors value it so highly, and why it is so controversial.

The short answer

Palantir sells software that pulls together an organisation’s scattered data, from databases, spreadsheets, sensors and documents, so people can analyse it and act on it. It does not collect or sell data itself; its customers bring their own. It makes money by selling that software, usually in large contracts, to two kinds of customer: governments, especially defence and intelligence agencies, and companies.

What makes it unusual is how it sells: for years it sent its own “forward-deployed engineers” to sit with customers and make the software work on their problems. And how it is valued: in 2025 its stock market value passed $400 billion, on revenue of under $3 billion the year before.

From PayPal to the Pentagon

  • 2003: founded in Palo Alto by Peter Thiel, Alex Karp, Stephen Cohen, Joe Lonsdale and Nathan Gettings. Thiel, a PayPal co-founder, said it could apply software similar to PayPal’s fraud detection to other problems. Karp, a former colleague of Thiel’s from Stanford Law School, became chief executive.
  • Early money: venture capital firms showed little interest, so Thiel and his fund largely paid for the initial $30 million. In-Q-Tel, the CIA-affiliated venture firm, invested about $2 million.
  • 2010s: its clients grew to include the CIA, the NSA, the FBI and the US military, which used it in Afghanistan to help predict where roadside bombs would be.
  • 2020: it went public on the New York Stock Exchange through a direct public offering rather than a traditional IPO.
  • 2023: it launched AIP, its Artificial Intelligence Platform, and reported its first profitable quarter under standard accounting rules (GAAP), $31 million, after about twenty years of losses.
  • 2024: it joined the S&P 500 and moved its listing to Nasdaq.

What it sells

ProductWho uses itWhat it does
GothamMilitaries, intelligence agencies, counter-terrorism analysts, some police forcesJoins data from many sources so analysts can find links, patterns and anomalies
FoundryCompanies and civil government, such as Airbus, Merck KGaA and NHS EnglandAn operating platform for an organisation’s data: integrate it, model it, build applications on it
ApolloPalantir’s own products, and customers’ secure environmentsDelivers and updates software continuously (CI/CD) across all of a customer’s environments
AIPBoth government and commercial customersBrings large language models into customers’ private networks, so they can build AI “agents” on their own data

Apollo’s software-as-a-service is one of five offerings the US Department of Defense has authorised for its most sensitive mission-critical systems (Impact Level 5).

How the money comes in

  • Large, long contracts. Government deals are often worth hundreds of millions: a nearly $1 billion software contract from the US Navy in November 2024, a seven-year, £330 million contract from NHS England in 2023 to run its Federated Data Platform, and a three-year, £240 million contract from the UK Ministry of Defence in December 2025.
  • Engineers on site. Its “forward-deployed engineers” work inside the customer’s organisation to connect the data and build what the customer needs. Analysis: it is expensive, but it ties the software closely to how the customer works.
  • Boot camps instead of long pilots. To sell AIP faster, Palantir runs five-day boot camps to onboard prospective customers.
  • Commercial growth. In the fourth quarter of 2025, revenue was $1.4 billion, up 70% on a year earlier; US commercial revenue rose 137% to $507 million, and total contract value signed in the quarter reached a record $4.3 billion.

Full-year 2025 figures are not shown because the sources we used disagree on them; the chart stops at 2024, where they agree.

How investors value it

Most companies are valued on their profits. Palantir’s price is far ahead of its profits, so investors are paying mainly for expected growth. Three numbers show the gap:

MeasureNumber
Market value in 2025, per The Economist$430 billion
That value divided by 2024 earnings ($462 million)over 600 times
Price against expected sales, November 202585 times forward annual sales, the most expensive in the S&P 500

Fortune noted the contrast in 2025: among the world’s most valuable companies, yet with revenue far below the $50 billion needed to join the Fortune 500. Compare Databricks, a private company valued at about 27 times its revenue, and see How Companies Are Valued for the methods.

Our view: the price only makes sense if AIP turns Palantir into the standard way large organisations run AI on their own data, and governments keep spending on defence software. The other view: The Economist called it possibly “the most over-valued firm of all time”; at these multiples, even strong growth may not be enough, and a slowdown would hit the share price hard.

Why it is controversial

Palantir’s customers include immigration enforcement, police forces and militaries, and its software can bring together a great deal of information about individuals. Civil liberties groups have raised concerns about mass surveillance; its use for predictive policing has drawn criticism over racial bias; and the NHS contract was criticised by the British Medical Association and others over patient privacy and procurement transparency.

The other view: Alex Karp disputes the surveillance characterisation, and supporters point out that Palantir does not collect or store data of its own: customers bring data they already hold, and keep the rights to it. They also point to uses such as fraud detection for the US government and the NHS’s COVID-19 vaccination programme. As with any powerful tool, the argument is less about the software than about who uses it, for what, and with what oversight.

Forward-deployed or self-serve? Choosing by requirement

Palantir’s sales model has been widely copied: The Wall Street Journal and Business Insider describe a “Palantir Mafia” of start-ups founded by former employees. If you are building a software business, which model fits depends on what is true for you:

If this is true for youIt leans towards
Few, very large customers with messy, sensitive dataForward-deployed engineers working on site
Customers in classified or disconnected networksDeployment tooling like Apollo, and certifications, before anything else
Many smaller customers who can set things up themselvesA self-serve product with good documentation
You need to prove value quickly to a sceptical buyerA short, hands-on boot camp on the customer’s own data
You cannot afford engineers per customer yetSelf-serve first; add on-site help for the biggest accounts

What engineers can take from it

  • Integrating messy data is where much of the value is; models are only as good as the data under them.
  • Engineers who sit with users learn what to build faster than any requirements document.
  • Who your software serves is a choice with consequences; the ethics are part of the engineering.
