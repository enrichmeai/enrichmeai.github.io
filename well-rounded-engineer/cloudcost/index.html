Guide / Cloud providers / Cloud Cost

Cloud cost

In the cloud, an engineer’s design choices become the monthly bill: the machine size, the region, where data is copied, how long it is kept. This page covers how cloud pricing works, the two kinds of lever for lowering a bill, how to see who spends what, and how to decide on commitments. Provider-by-provider discount names are on GCP vs AWS vs Azure; when multi-cloud pays off is on Retail & Hybrid Cloud.

The short answer

Our view: make cost visible to the people who make the design choices, and treat it as one requirement among several, next to reliability, security and speed. The other view is in the card “Our view, and the other view”.

FinOps, and what the providers say

Google describes cloud FinOps (financial operations) as an operational framework and cultural practice that brings engineering, finance and business teams together to manage cloud spending and the business value it buys; rather than simply cutting costs, it sets up shared accountability for spending that varies with use.

All three providers publish cost guidance in their Well-Architected Frameworks. Among Google’s principles: align spending with business value, make people aware of the cost of their decisions, use only what you need, and optimise continuously. AWS’s cost pillar includes adopting a consumption model and measuring the business output of a workload against what it costs to deliver.

How cloud pricing works

  • On demand: pay for what you use, with no commitment.
  • Commitments: a lower price for agreeing to use, or spend, a minimum amount for one or three years: Google’s committed use discounts, AWS Savings Plans and Azure reservations. AWS and Azure each say up to 72%; Google publishes its rates product by product.
  • Spot capacity: spare capacity at a much lower price, which the provider can take back. Google quotes discounts of up to 91% for Spot VMs; AWS gives a two-minute warning before stopping a Spot Instance, and Azure 30 seconds before evicting a Spot VM.
  • Storage classes: colder classes cost less to keep and more to read. In Cloud Storage, Nearline, Coldline and Archive have minimum storage durations of 30, 90 and 365 days.
  • Data transfer: Google charges nothing for inbound data, but moving data between zones in one region is charged per GiB, and leaving the cloud costs more; check each provider’s price list.

Two kinds of lever

Worked example: when does a commitment pay off?

A commitment is paid whether or not you use it. With a discount of d, it is cheaper than paying on demand only if you use more than 1 − d of what you committed to. Switching off a machine that is needed only in office hours is a usage lever with no commitment at all: AWS’s cost pillar uses the example of development and test environments used eight hours a day in the working week, 40 hours of 168, and puts the potential saving at 75%.

# Is a commitment cheaper than paying on demand?
# You pay for the commitment whether you use it or not.

def cheaper_to_commit(discount, used):
    on_demand = used            # pay only for what you use (price 1.0)
    committed = 1 - discount    # pay the discounted price for all of it
    return committed < on_demand

for discount in (0.20, 0.40, 0.60):
    break_even = 1 - discount
    print(f"discount {discount:.0%}: commit if you use more than {break_even:.0%};",
          "at 70% use:", "commit" if cheaper_to_commit(discount, 0.70) else "on demand")

hours_on, hours_week = 40, 24 * 7
print(f"office-hours dev box: runs {hours_on} of {hours_week} hours,",
      f"so switching it off saves {1 - hours_on / hours_week:.0%}")

discount 20%: commit if you use more than 80%; at 70% use: on demand
discount 40%: commit if you use more than 60%; at 70% use: commit
discount 60%: commit if you use more than 40%; at 70% use: commit
office-hours dev box: runs 40 of 168 hours, so switching it off saves 76%

Run as shown with Python 3; the second block is its real output. The discounts are round numbers for the arithmetic, not any provider’s price. AWS gives 75%.

Seeing who spends what

  • Tags and labels: AWS cost allocation tags, once activated, group costs on the cost allocation report by each tag’s key and value; Google Cloud passes resource labels, such as a team or cost centre, to billing, so charges can be grouped by label.
  • Budgets and alerts: Google warns that an alerts-only budget does not cap usage or spending; it only tells you how costs are trending. Stopping spend needs an action, which can be automated, for example by disabling billing on a project, or a spend-cap budget where the service supports one.
  • One format for cost data: FOCUS, the FinOps Open Cost and Usage Specification, is an open schema for cost and usage data across clouds, SaaS and data centres, so bills from different providers can be compared.

Cost as an engineering choice

Analysis: some of the largest costs are fixed by design rather than by tuning: a chatty service in another zone or region pays for every byte it moves; a copy of data kept “just in case” is paid for every month; logs kept at full detail for ever grow without limit. These are cheapest to fix while the design is still on paper.

Azure’s cost guidance also warns against choosing a cheaper option that weakens security, scalability, resilience or operability, because that can cost the business more later.

Choose by requirement

If this is true for youIt leans towards (analysis)
Steady load that will run for a year or moreA commitment, sized to the base you are confident of, with on demand above it
Batch or stateless work that can be stopped and retriedSpot capacity, with the work built to handle the notice period
Load that rises and falls with usersAutoscaling, or serverless that scales to zero, before any commitment
Environments needed only in working hoursA schedule that switches them off
Data read rarely but kept for yearsA colder storage class, with lifecycle rules; check the minimum storage duration and retrieval cost
Services that call each other heavilyPlace them, and their data, in the same region, and the same zone where resilience allows
Nobody can say which team owns a costTags or labels, applied by policy, before any optimisation
Your team already has commitments or tooling with one providerUse them first: moving has its own cost

Our view, and the other view

Our view: engineers should see the cost of what they build, per service and per unit of business output, as AWS’s pillar suggests, and design with it from the start.

The other view: early in a product, speed of learning matters more than the bill, and time spent tuning cost can be time not spent finding out whether anyone wants the product; on this view, cost work waits until the spend is large enough to justify it.

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Read a cloud bill and name its biggest lines
  • Tag or label what you create
  • Switch off what you are not using

2 Practitioner

  • Right-size and autoscale a service
  • Set budgets and alerts, knowing alerts-only budgets do not cap spend
  • Pick a storage class by how often data is read

3 Senior

  • Decide on commitments from measured base load
  • Use spot capacity for interruptible work
  • Design data placement to limit transfer costs

4 Expert

  • Build a FinOps practice across engineering and finance
  • Measure cost per unit of business output
  • Weigh cost against reliability and security

Each level includes the ones before it. See Start here for what the levels mean.
