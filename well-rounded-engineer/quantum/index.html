Guide / Frontiers / Quantum Computing

Quantum computing, in plain words

Quantum computers are not faster versions of ordinary computers. They are a different kind of machine that is very good at a few specific problems and no help at most others. For engineers today, the most practical part is not building quantum programs: it is preparing cryptography for the day large quantum computers exist.

The core ideas

  • A qubit is the quantum version of a bit. Before it is measured it can be in a combination (a superposition) of 0 and 1; measuring it gives 0 or 1 with certain probabilities.
  • Entanglement links qubits so that their measurement results are correlated in ways ordinary bits cannot be.
  • Interference is how quantum algorithms work: they arrange for wrong answers to cancel out and right answers to reinforce, so that measuring gives a useful result.
  • Noise is the hard part. Qubits are fragile; today's machines make errors, and correcting them needs many physical qubits for each reliable one.

What it is good for, and what it is not

  • Promising: simulating molecules and materials, some optimisation and sampling problems, and certain mathematical problems such as factoring large numbers. In finance, a technique called quantum amplitude estimation could in principle speed up Monte Carlo risk calculations; today that is research and pilots, not production.
  • Not useful for: running websites, databases, everyday apps or most business software. Ordinary computers stay better at almost everything.
  • Be wary of hype: claims of quantum advantage are usually about narrow, carefully chosen problems.

Shor's algorithm in simple terms

Multiplying two primes is easy: 61 × 53 = 3,233. Going back, finding which primes make 3,233, is hard, and for the very large numbers used in RSA it is impractical for ordinary computers. Shor turned factoring into finding a repeating pattern. Here it is for 15:

  1. Pick a number, say 7, and take its powers, keeping only the remainder after dividing by 15: 7, 4, 13, 1, 7, 4, 13, 1…
  2. The pattern repeats every 4 steps, so the period is 4.
  3. Take half the period: 7² − 1 = 48 and 7² + 1 = 50.
  4. What each shares with 15: 48 and 15 share 3; 50 and 15 share 5. So 15 = 3 × 5.

Every step is easy for an ordinary computer except finding the period when the number has hundreds of digits. That is the one step the quantum computer does: it puts many powers into superposition, computes them together, and uses interference (the quantum Fourier transform) so that the period stands out when measured. The rest runs on an ordinary computer, so it is a hybrid of the two.

The numbers factored this way on real quantum hardware are still tiny. Breaking RSA needs a large, error-corrected machine that does not exist yet, which is why the move to post-quantum cryptography is happening now, before it does.

Why it matters for security: post-quantum cryptography

  • Shor's algorithm, on a large, error-corrected quantum computer, could break the public-key cryptography the internet relies on today (RSA and elliptic-curve keys and signatures).
  • "Harvest now, decrypt later": someone can record encrypted traffic today and decrypt it once such a machine exists, so data that must stay secret for many years is already at risk.
  • New standards exist. NIST has standardised post-quantum algorithms: ML-KEM (FIPS 203) for key exchange, based on Kyber, and ML-DSA (FIPS 204) and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) for digital signatures.
  • Symmetric cryptography holds up better: Grover's algorithm only gives a quadratic speed-up, so longer keys (such as AES-256) remain a sound choice.
  • What to do now: know where your systems use public-key cryptography (TLS, signing, key exchange); keep crypto libraries current, since many are adding post-quantum and hybrid options; design so algorithms can be swapped (crypto agility); and plan the move for long-lived secrets first.

Try it yourself

Qiskit is an open-source SDK for building quantum circuits in Python and running them on simulators on your laptop. A good first exercise: build a circuit that entangles two qubits (a Bell state), run it many times on the simulator, and see that the two results always agree.

Keep your feet on the ground

You do not need quantum physics to be ready. You need to know where your cryptography lives, keep it up to date, and be able to change it. That is good engineering whether quantum computers arrive soon or late.

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Explain qubits, superposition and entanglement in plain words
  • Say what quantum computers are and are not good for
  • Explain why Shor's algorithm threatens RSA and elliptic curves

2 Practitioner

  • Inventory where your systems use public-key cryptography
  • Keep crypto libraries current and follow their post-quantum options
  • Build and run a simple circuit on a simulator

3 Senior

  • Plan a post-quantum migration, long-lived secrets first
  • Design for crypto agility
  • Judge vendor quantum claims critically

4 Expert

  • Own the organisation's post-quantum roadmap
  • Advise on where quantum computing could genuinely help
  • Track standards and research and translate them for teams

Each level includes the ones before it. See Start here for what the levels mean.
