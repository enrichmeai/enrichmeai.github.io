Mathematics, for life and for engineering

It starts with the everyday maths every person should know, for money, risk and reading the news, then the mathematics that keeps showing up in real engineering work: logic and discrete maths, how algorithms scale, probability and statistics, the linear algebra behind embeddings, how computers store numbers, and quick estimation. Not to pass an exam, but to reason precisely and catch what looks right and is not.

Everyday mathematics, for everyone

You do not need advanced maths to live well, but a handful of ideas protect your money, your health and your judgment every day. None of this is about being "good at maths"; it is about not being fooled, including by yourself.

Percentages and money

  • Up and down are not equal. A price that rises 50% and then falls 50% ends at 75% of where it started (1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75). Down 20% then up 20% leaves you at 96%.
  • Percent is not percentage points. An interest rate going from 2% to 3% is up one percentage point, but up 50% in percent terms. Watch which one you are being told.
  • Quick mental maths: 10% is the number with the decimal point moved one place; 5% is half of that; 15% is the two added; 1% is the point moved two places.
  • Compare unit prices, per kilo or per litre, not pack prices. The bigger pack is not always cheaper.
  • Small amounts add up: £4 a day is £1,460 a year, and £14,600 over ten years, before any interest.

Compound growth, for you and against you

  • Compounding means growth on growth. £1,000 at 5% a year becomes about £1,629 after ten years if the interest is reinvested, against £1,500 if it is not.
  • The rule of 72: divide 72 by the yearly rate to estimate how many years it takes to double. At 6% about 12 years (exactly 11.9); at 3% about 24 (exactly 23.4).
  • Debt compounds too. A balance at 24% a year, left unpaid, doubles in about 3 years (72 ÷ 24). Pay off the most expensive debt first.
  • Inflation is compounding in reverse: at 3% a year, money left in a drawer loses half its buying power in about 24 years.
  • Start early. Time does more of the work than the amount: small, regular savings started young often end up ahead of larger ones started late.

Risk and chance

  • Relative risk can mislead. "Doubles your risk" may mean going from 1 in 10,000 to 2 in 10,000. Always ask: out of how many?
  • Base rates matter. Suppose a test is 99% accurate both ways and the condition affects 1 person in 1,000. Of everyone who tests positive, only about 9% actually have it, because the false positives from the 999 healthy people outnumber the true ones.
  • Expected value: multiply each outcome by its chance and add them up. Lotteries and most casino games pay back less than you put in, on average, by design.
  • Streaks happen by chance. A coin that lands heads five times is not "due" tails; each toss is independent.

Reading numbers in the news

  • Average or median? A few very high incomes pull the average up; the median (the middle value) is often a fairer picture of a typical person.
  • Correlation is not causation. Two things rising together may share a third cause, or be coincidence.
  • Check the chart's axis. A bar chart whose axis does not start at zero can make a small difference look huge.
  • Ask for the size of the sample and who was asked. A survey of 20 people, or only of fans, says little.
  • Feel the size of big numbers: a million seconds is about 11.6 days; a billion seconds is about 31.7 years.

The jump from O(n log n) to O(n²) is the difference between a second and days. An N+1 query pattern and a nested loop over two big lists are the usual ways it sneaks in.

Numbers computers get "wrong"

>>> 0.1 + 0.2
0.30000000000000004              # binary floating point cannot store 0.1 exactly
>>> 0.1 + 0.2 == 0.3
False
>>> from decimal import Decimal
>>> Decimal("0.1") + Decimal("0.2") == Decimal("0.3")
True                             # decimal arithmetic for money

// Java: int overflow wraps around silently
int max = Integer.MAX_VALUE;     // 2,147,483,647
max + 1                          // -2,147,483,648
Math.addExact(max, 1)            // throws ArithmeticException instead
  • Use decimal types or integer minor units (paise, cents) for money.
  • Never compare floats with ==; compare within a tolerance.
  • Know your integer sizes: a signed 32-bit counter of milliseconds overflows in under 25 days (unsigned, about 50).

Statistics that keep you honest

  • Median and percentiles beat the mean for skewed data such as latency: one slow request moves the mean, not the median.
  • Variance matters: two systems with the same average can have very different worst cases.
  • Sample size: a difference seen in 20 requests may be noise; in 20,000 it is more likely real.
  • Base rates (Bayes): a 99% accurate alert for a problem that happens 1 time in 10,000 fires falsely about 100 times for each real case.
  • Correlation is not causation: two metrics rising together may share a cause.
  • Survivorship: you only see the systems, users or rows that made it through.
  • Multiple comparisons: test twenty things at 95% confidence and you should expect about one "significant" result by chance.

Probability in operations

# availability of dependencies in series multiplies
0.999 * 0.999 * 0.999           # ≈ 0.997  (three 99.9% services)

# chance at least one of n calls is slow, if each is slow with p = 0.01
1 - (1 - 0.01) ** 100           # ≈ 0.63

# birthday paradox: random 32-bit IDs collide sooner than you think
import math
math.sqrt(2 ** 32) * 1.1774     # ≈ 77,000 IDs for a 50% chance of a collision

The birthday bound is why IDs are 128-bit UUIDs or longer, and why hash collisions are planned for, not assumed away.

Linear algebra behind embeddings

  • An embedding is a vector: a list of numbers that places a text in a space where similar meanings are close.
  • Dot product: multiply matching entries and add them up.
  • Cosine similarity = dot product ÷ (length of a × length of b): 1 means same direction, 0 unrelated.
  • For vectors already normalised to length 1, cosine similarity and dot product are the same, which is why many vector databases normalise first.
import numpy as np
a, b = np.array([0.2, 0.8, 0.1]), np.array([0.25, 0.7, 0.0])
cosine = a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))   # ≈ 0.99: very similar

  • Round to one significant figure and multiply orders of magnitude: being within 2× is good enough to choose a design.
  • Check units at every step; most estimation errors are unit errors.
  • Sanity-check against something known: a day is about 10⁵ seconds; a year about 3 × 10⁷.

How to learn it as an adult engineer

  • Learn each topic through a problem you have: statistics through your latency data, graphs through your DAG, linear algebra through your embeddings.
  • Work examples by hand on paper, then check them in Python.
  • Use AI as a patient tutor: ask for an explanation, then for exercises, then check your answers yourself.
  • Fifteen minutes a day beats a weekend once a year.

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Work out percentages, compound growth and risk in daily life
  • Read Big-O and spot a nested loop over large data
  • Use median and percentiles rather than averages
  • Know why floats are wrong for money

2 Practitioner

  • Reason about complexity when choosing data structures and queries
  • Apply basic probability to retries, availability and alerts
  • Estimate capacity and cost with orders of magnitude

3 Senior

  • Design experiments and read their statistics critically
  • Use linear algebra and statistics confidently in data and ML work
  • Prove or argue correctness of algorithms and migrations

4 Expert

  • Bring mathematical rigour to architecture and research decisions
  • Teach the maths behind the team's systems
  • Spot subtle statistical and numerical errors in others' work
