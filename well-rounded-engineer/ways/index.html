Ways of working: commits, tickets, Agile and XP

The habits that decide whether a team is fast or merely busy: a commit history people can read, tickets people can build from, updates that show the real state, and a pace that lasts. Where a claim comes from a spec or a source it is linked at the end; where it is opinion from years of working in teams, it says so.

feat, fix and chore: Conventional Commits

The Conventional Commits spec gives every commit message a type in front: type(optional scope): description. The point is a history a person can scan and a tool can read, for example to work out the next version number or write release notes.

TypeUse it whenEffect on the version (Semantic Versioning)
feat:Something a user of the code can now do that they could not beforeMINOR
fix:A bug is patched: the code now does what it already claimed to doPATCH
feat!: or a BREAKING CHANGE: footerExisting users must change something (any type can carry the !)MAJOR
chore:Housekeeping that changes nothing a user sees: dependency bumps, tidying confignone
docs: refactor: test: perf: build: ci: style:What the name says. These come from the widely used commitlint convention (based on Angular's), not from the spec itselfnone, unless marked breaking
feat(search): filter results by court
fix(login): stop the session expiring after 5 minutes
chore: bump test dependencies
refactor(api)!: rename /cases to /judgments

BREAKING CHANGE: clients must call /judgments

How to choose: ask "what would a user of this code notice?" A new capability is feat. Wrong behaviour made right is fix. Nothing noticeable is chore, refactor, docs or test. If a commit needs two types, it is usually two commits.

Writing a ticket someone can build from

  • Start with the why. The common user story shape is "As a <role> I can <capability>, so that <benefit>". The "so that" is the part people skip and the part that matters most.
  • Acceptance criteria: a short list of things that are true when it is done, each one checkable. "Given a signed-in user, when they search for X, then they see Y."
  • Context: links to the error, the log line, the screenshot, the conversation. The reader should not have to find you to start.
  • Out of scope: say what this ticket will not do, so it does not quietly grow.
  • INVEST (Bill Wake): Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable. If it fails "Small", split it.
  • A bug ticket has steps to reproduce, what you expected, what happened, and where (version, environment, browser).

What Agile and XP actually said

The Agile Manifesto (17 practitioners, 2001) values:

  • individuals and interactions over processes and tools
  • working software over comprehensive documentation
  • customer collaboration over contract negotiation
  • responding to change over following a plan

Extreme Programming (Kent Beck, from the Chrysler C3 payroll project) named four values in 1999: communication, simplicity, feedback and courage. Respect was added in the second edition. Its practices include test-first, pair programming, continuous integration, small releases, and a sustainable pace: one of its rules is simply "no overtime".

Honest updates: don't give an update and hide

  • The daily scrum is meant to be under 15 minutes, to show progress toward the goal and the issues in the way. Its job is to surface problems, not to perform being busy.
  • A burndown chart is described as a publicly displayed chart of remaining work. Public is the point: the real state, visible to everyone, every day.
  • Say "blocked" the moment you are, and on what. A blocker raised at 10am costs an hour; raised at the sprint review it costs the sprint.
  • "Nearly done" is not a status. Say what is done, what is left, and what you are unsure about.
  • Bad news early is a plan; bad news late is an incident. Teams that punish the messenger get green dashboards and red launches.
  • Show, don't tell: a link to the PR, the test run, the demo, beats a sentence about progress.

Continuous proactiveness: not every day is the same

  • Not every day is 9 to 5. An incident at night, a launch, a customer stuck on a deadline: some days need more, and a good engineer shows up for them.
  • Don’t be complacent. Keep perspective: a hard day at a keyboard is not as hard as a shift in a coal mine. Most of this work is safe, warm and well paid. That is a reason to give it full effort, not to coast.
  • Proactive means you see it before you are told: the alert that is noisy, the doc that is wrong, the dependency about to expire, the colleague who is stuck.
  • But the long-run pace is sustainable. XP's "no overtime" rule is about the normal week: a team that runs every week on heroics stops noticing its own mistakes. A hard day is followed by recovery, not by another hard day.
  • Make the hard days rarer: after every late night, ask what would have prevented it, and fix that (a test, an alert, a runbook).
  • Flexible both ways: giving extra on the day it is needed earns the quiet afternoon when it is not.

When Agile slows a company down

This card is opinion and observation from working in many teams, not a measured study. Agile was meant to make teams faster. In many companies it has done the opposite, and the pattern is usually the same: the ceremonies were kept and the values were dropped.

What the manifesto saidWhat often happens instead
Individuals and interactions over processes and toolsThe process and the ticket tool become the work: estimation meetings, refinement of refinement, fields nobody reads
Working software over comprehensive documentationStatus reports and slide decks about the sprint, while nothing ships for weeks
Customer collaborationThe team never meets a user; requirements arrive through three layers of proxies
Responding to changeA fixed quarterly plan, renamed a "roadmap", with sprints as deadlines
Small, frequent releasesTwo-week sprints that end in a release train once a quarter

The way back is small: ship something real more often, put the people who build next to the people who use it, cut any meeting whose output nobody uses, and measure delivery (how often you release, how long a change takes to reach users) rather than story points.
