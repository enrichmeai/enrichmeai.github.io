Electronics and chips: the bottom of the stack

Every line of code runs on transistors. Understanding how a chip is built, and how one could be designed, keeps the foundations open to everyone rather than to a few companies.

Why this belongs in a software guide

A new generation of engineers needs the whole foundation, not only the top layers. Today a small number of companies design and make most of the world's advanced chips: TSMC alone accounts for roughly 70% of the global foundry market. When knowledge sits in so few places, everyone else depends on them for every device, data centre and AI model.

Learning electronics does not mean everyone builds a fab. It means more people understand what their software runs on, can repair and reuse hardware instead of throwing it away, and can take part in open hardware. That is how the foundations stay shared, and how technology becomes more sustainable.

From switch to chip

  • The transistor is an electrically controlled switch. The first, a point-contact transistor, was invented in 1947 at Bell Labs by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley, who shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics.
  • Logic gates (AND, OR, NOT) are built from transistors; everything a computer does is built from gates.
  • The integrated circuit puts many transistors on one piece of semiconductor: credited to Jack Kilby (1958) and Robert Noyce (1959).
  • Moore's law is the observation that the number of transistors on a chip doubles about every two years, which drove decades of cheaper, faster computing.

How chips are designed

  • Hardware description languages such as Verilog and VHDL describe circuits as code, which is then tested in simulation like software.
  • FPGAs are chips that can be reprogrammed after manufacture: the best way to try your own digital designs on real hardware without making a chip.
  • Instruction sets define what a processor understands. RISC-V is a free and open standard instruction set, started at the University of California, Berkeley: anyone can design a RISC-V processor without paying royalties.
  • Open manufacturing kits exist too: SkyWater's 130 nm process design kit (SKY130) is available under the Apache License, so a chip design can be taken to real silicon with open tools.

Sustainability

  • E-waste is one of the fastest-growing waste streams: about 62 million tonnes in 2022, of which only 22.3% was formally collected and recycled; it is projected to reach 82 million tonnes by 2030.
  • Repair before replace. The right-to-repair movement argues that people should be able to fix what they own. Engineers who understand electronics can design for repair and keep devices in use longer.
  • Efficient software is green software. Code that does less work needs less hardware and less power. See Performance and Body, Mind & Nature.

Where to start

  1. Learn basic circuits: voltage, current, resistance, and how a transistor switches.
  2. Build logic gates and a small adder in a free simulator, then in Verilog.
  3. Program a microcontroller board to read a sensor and drive a motor. See Robotics.
  4. Put a small design on an FPGA board, then a simple RISC-V core.
  5. Read how chips are made, and repair one device instead of replacing it.

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Explain voltage, current and how a transistor switches
  • Explain logic gates and how they add numbers
  • Explain what an instruction set is

2 Practitioner

  • Build a microcontroller project with sensors and motors
  • Write and simulate a small design in Verilog or VHDL
  • Run a design on an FPGA board

3 Senior

  • Design a simple processor or peripheral on an FPGA
  • Read datasheets and choose components for a product
  • Design hardware for repair and long life

4 Expert

  • Lead hardware and software co-design
  • Take a design towards silicon with an open process kit
  • Shape open hardware and sustainability practice

Each level includes the ones before it. See Start here for what the levels mean.
