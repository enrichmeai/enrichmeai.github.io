Routing and trains with neural networks

How Google Maps uses a graph neural network to predict travel times, why the route itself is still found by a classic algorithm, how trains are actually controlled today, and where neural networks are, and are not, being trusted on the railway.

The short answer

Neural networks are very good at one part of routing: predicting. How long will this road take at 8 a.m.? How late will this train be? They are much less trusted at the part that keeps people safe: deciding that two trains must never be on the same piece of track. So in practice the two are combined: a learned model predicts, and a well-understood algorithm or certified safety system decides.

If neural networks are new to you, start with Neural Networks; for networks of connected things, see Graph Databases.

Road routing: what Google Maps does

Google Maps combines live traffic with historical patterns to predict travel times. Working with Google DeepMind, it improved those predictions using a graph neural network (GNN), a network designed for data shaped like a graph: places joined by roads.

  • The problem: Maps’ predicted arrival times were already accurate for over 97% of trips, according to DeepMind. The goal was the remaining few per cent.
  • Supersegments: roads are divided into “supersegments”, groups of adjacent road segments that share significant traffic. A first attempt trained a separate ordinary neural network for every supersegment; because supersegments vary in size, that did not scale, so the team switched to a GNN, which can model how traffic flows from one road to the next.
  • The result: DeepMind reported accuracy improvements of up to 50% in cities including Berlin, Jakarta, São Paulo, Sydney, Tokyo and Washington D.C. The research paper, presented at CIKM 2021, reports that the deployed model cut negative arrival-time outcomes by over 40% in cities like Sydney, compared with the previous production system.

The predicted times then become the weights on the road graph, and a shortest-path search finds the fastest route. The neural network makes the map smarter; it does not replace the route search.

Run it: learning predicts, the algorithm decides

A small version of that split. A learned estimate of each road’s travel time (here a simple average by time of day, standing in for a neural network) feeds Dijkstra’s algorithm, the classic way to find the shortest path in a graph:

# Learning predicts, the algorithm decides: how map routing combines the two.
import heapq

# 1. Past trips: (road, hour of day, minutes taken). Real systems learn from billions.
history = [("ring", 8, 22), ("ring", 9, 20), ("ring", 14, 9), ("ring", 15, 8),
           ("centre", 8, 12), ("centre", 9, 13), ("centre", 14, 11), ("centre", 15, 12)]

# 2. "Learn" each road's travel time for a time of day. This tiny lookup by rush hour
#    stands in for the graph neural network Google Maps uses.
def predict(road, hour):
    rush = 7 <= hour <= 9
    times = [m for r, h, m in history if r == road and (7 <= h <= 9) == rush]
    return sum(times) / len(times)

# 3. The road network, with each road's model input. Two ways from Home to Office.
roads = {"Home": [("A", "ring"), ("B", "centre")], "A": [("Office", "ring")],
         "B": [("Office", "centre")], "Office": []}

# 4. Dijkstra's algorithm finds the fastest route using the predicted times as weights.
def fastest(start, goal, hour):
    queue, seen = [(0.0, start, [start])], set()
    while queue:
        minutes, node, path = heapq.heappop(queue)
        if node == goal:
            return round(minutes), path
        if node in seen:
            continue
        seen.add(node)
        for nxt, road in roads[node]:
            heapq.heappush(queue, (minutes + predict(road, hour), nxt, path + [nxt]))

for hour in (8, 14):
    print(f"{hour:02}:00 ->", fastest("Home", "Office", hour))

What it printed when we ran it:

08:00 -> (25, ['Home', 'B', 'Office'])
14:00 -> (17, ['Home', 'A', 'Office'])

At 8 a.m. the ring road is slow, so the route goes through the centre; at 2 p.m. the ring road is quicker. The route search never changed; only the predicted travel times did. That is the job a neural network does inside a routing system.

How trains are controlled today

Running trains safely is mostly about keeping them apart, and that is done with signalling and train protection systems, not learning:

  • Blocks. Traditional signalling divides track into fixed blocks; only one train may be in a block at a time. Communications-based train control (CBTC) knows each train’s position more precisely by radio and uses a moving block that travels with the train, so trains can run closer together safely. The first radio-based CBTC opened at San Francisco airport’s people mover in 2003.
  • Movement authority. In the European Train Control System (ETCS), a Radio Block Centre, specified to safety integrity level 4 (SIL 4), the highest, generates the movement authorities that tell each train how far it may go.
  • Automatic train operation. Driving can be automated in grades: in many systems (GoA2) the train drives itself but a driver is on board, as on London Underground’s Victoria line; at GoA4, trains run with no staff on board at all. Driverless operation is mostly used on lines with isolated tracks, where safety is easier to ensure; fully automated mainline railways are still an area of research.

Where learning is being tried: Flatland

The hard, unsolved part of running a railway is rescheduling: when a train breaks down, which of hundreds of trains should wait, re-route or go first? Swiss Federal Railways (SBB) runs the densest mixed railway traffic in the world, with more than 10,000 trains a day routed over 13,000 switches and controlled by more than 32,000 signals.

To open the problem to researchers, Flatland, an open-source toolkit run as a challenge on the AIcrowd platform and built around SBB’s problem, offers a simplified grid-world railway where many trains (agents) must reach their stations. It lets people test multi-agent reinforcement learning, where each train learns by trial and error, against traditional operations research, the planning and optimisation methods railways have used for decades.

The honest result so far: in the 2020 Flatland competition, the best submissions came from operations research, though participants found many promising reinforcement-learning approaches, often using graph representations of the network. The organisers concluded that machine learning has potential for the problem and that key topics still need research.

Choosing an approach, by requirement

If this is true for youIt leans towards
You need predictions from lots of messy historical data: travel times, delays, demandA neural network, often a graph neural network for networks
You need the best route given known costsA classic algorithm such as Dijkstra or A*, fed by those predictions
A wrong decision could injure peopleDeterministic, certified safety systems; learning may advise but not decide
You must replan many vehicles after a disruption, todayOperations research methods, which still won the Flatland benchmark
You are researching what could work nextReinforcement learning in a simulator such as Flatland, compared against operations research

Our view, and the other view

Our view: the winning pattern is a partnership. Let neural networks predict, let proven algorithms plan, and keep certified systems in charge of safety. That is how Google Maps works, and how railways are likely to adopt learning.

The other view: some researchers argue learned systems will eventually plan and control better than hand-built ones, as they have in games, and that insisting on old methods holds progress back. The evidence today does not yet show that on real railways.
