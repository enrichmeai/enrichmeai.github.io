Chip jobs and GPU startups

AI runs on chips, and the world is short of people who can design, make and program them. What the jobs are, what the numbers say, whether AI will end up designing chips itself, and, for anyone with an electronics background and a bigger ambition, what it really takes to start a company that makes GPUs.

The short answer

For twenty years the career advice for engineers pointed one way: towards software. Hardware looked slow, expensive and finished. AI has changed that. Every model is trained and served on chips, the companies that make them have become some of the most valuable in the world, and governments are spending large sums to build chip industries at home. That creates jobs at every level, from technicians in a fab to engineers who write the code that runs on a GPU.

If you studied electronics, you already hold the foundation many software engineers lack: you know what a transistor, a clock and a signal really are. The rest of this page is about how to build on that, whether you want a job or a company. If binary, gates and chip design are still hazy, start with From Binary to GPUs.

Why chips are a job for the AI era

The clearest numbers come from the United States, where the Semiconductor Industry Association commissioned a study from Oxford Economics in 2023. It projected that the industry’s US workforce would grow from about 345,000 jobs to about 460,000 by 2030, and that roughly 67,000 of the new jobs risk going unfilled at current graduation rates. Of those unfilled jobs, 39% are technicians, most with certificates or two-year degrees; 35% are engineers with four-year degrees or computer scientists; and 26% are engineers at master’s or PhD level. So this is not only a field for PhDs.

Governments are paying for the build-out:

  • United States: the CHIPS and Science Act (2022) includes $39 billion in subsidies for chip manufacturing.
  • India: the Semicon India Programme began with an outlay of ₹76,000 crore for fabs, packaging plants and a Design Linked Incentive scheme for chip design companies. Its next phase, Semicon 2.0, was approved with ₹1,27,500 crore, and talent development is one of its pillars.
  • Europe has its own European Chips Act.

Analysis: subsidies build factories, but factories need people, and people take years to train. That gap is the opportunity for anyone willing to learn now.

The jobs, layer by layer

“Working in chips” covers very different work. Some of it is about making the chip; some of it is about programming a chip someone else made. Here is the map, from the factory floor up:

RoleWhat you doWhat to learn
Fab or test technicianRun and maintain the equipment that makes and tests wafersA certificate or two-year course; cleanroom practice; the equipment itself
Design engineer (RTL)Describe the chip’s logic in Verilog, SystemVerilog or VHDLDigital logic, computer architecture, a hardware description language
Verification engineerProve the design does what the spec says, before it is manufacturedSimulation, test benches, coverage; a lot like testing software, with higher stakes
Physical design engineerTurn logic into a layout: placement, routing, timing, powerEDA tools, timing analysis, the foundry’s process rules
FPGA engineerProgram a chip whose logic can be rewired after manufactureThe same HDLs; high-level synthesis lets you start from C or C++
GPU kernel programmerWrite the code that makes AI models run fast on GPUsParallel programming, CUDA, or Triton, which lets you write GPU kernels as Python functions
Compiler and tools engineerBuild the software that turns models and designs into something a chip can runCompilers, computer architecture, and usually a lot of C++

Analysis: the bottom rows are where software engineers cross over most easily. Triton, for example, was released by OpenAI in 2021 and is now the default GPU code generator behind PyTorch’s torch.compile. The top rows are where an electronics degree counts most.

Will AI design the chips?

It already helps, and the honest picture is more interesting than the headlines.

  • Commercial tools use it. Synopsys, a chip design software company listed in 2024 as the world’s 12th largest software company, introduced DSO.ai in 2020, which uses reinforcement learning to make logic synthesis and place-and-route decisions; it added AI tools for verification and test in 2023, and a generative AI assistant built with Microsoft the same year.
  • The famous research result is disputed. In 2021 Google researchers reported in Nature that a reinforcement learning system, later called AlphaChip, placed parts of a chip “in under six hours” and beat human-made layouts, and Google used it in designing its TPUs. Since then, independent researchers at UC San Diego re-implemented the method and found it did not consistently beat established placement methods; in their comparisons it never produced shorter wiring than a commercial placer. In 2023 Nature added an editor’s note saying the claims had been called into question, and the accompanying commentary was retracted. Google’s authors reject the criticism.

Our view: designing chips still needs human intelligence today: deciding what to build, what to trade away, and what “good enough” means for a product. But at some point AI is going to design chips. The steps are already being automated one at a time, from placement to verification, and each one that falls makes the next easier.

The other view: chip design is unforgiving in a way most software is not. A mistake found after manufacture can cost a new mask set and months, so the industry adopts new methods slowly and demands proof; the AlphaChip dispute shows how hard that proof is to get. On this view AI becomes a powerful assistant inside the tools, while people stay accountable for the design for a long time yet.

Analysis: either way, the safest career bet is the same: understand the fundamentals well enough to direct the tools and to check their answers.

Starting a GPU company: the bottlenecks

Suppose you want to build a company that makes GPUs or AI accelerators. The good news first: you do not need your own factory. Almost every chip company today, Nvidia included, is fabless: it designs chips and pays a foundry to make them. That is just as well, because a modern fab costs tens of billions of dollars.

Even without a factory, five things stand in the way:

  • Software, the biggest moat. A GPU is only useful if people’s code runs on it. Nvidia released CUDA in 2007, and nearly two decades of libraries, tools and habits have grown around it. A new chip needs drivers, a compiler and libraries good enough that customers do not have to rewrite their models.
  • Money for each attempt. A complete mask set can need as many as 30 masks, and each attempt at an advanced process means a new set and wafers before you know whether the chip works.
  • Manufacturing that a few companies control. Leading-edge chips are made by a small number of foundries, TSMC above all, and ASML is the world’s only manufacturer of the EUV lithography machines those foundries need for the most advanced chips.
  • Memory and packaging. AI chips need high-bandwidth memory (HBM), made by SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron, joined to the chip with advanced packaging that is itself in short supply.
  • Rules and people. Export controls decide who may buy what: in 2023 the US added the Chinese GPU maker Moore Threads to its Entity List. And the engineers who have taken a big chip to production before are few and much sought after.

What happened to the challengers

Real companies show how these bottlenecks play out:

CompanyWhat it triedWhat happened
NvidiaFounded in 1993 by three engineers; released the GeForce 256, the first product it marketed as a GPU, in 1999Built CUDA in 2007 and became the company everyone else is measured against
GraphcoreA new kind of processor for AI, founded in 2016 in the UKValued at $1.7 billion in 2018; SoftBank agreed to buy it in 2024 for around $500 million
GroqAn inference chip, founded in 2016 by former Google engineers including one of the TPU’s designersIn December 2025 Nvidia agreed to license its technology in a deal reportedly worth about $20 billion
CerebrasOne chip the size of a whole wafer, to avoid the cost of connecting many GPUsRevenue of $510 million in 2025, though 62% of it came from one customer
TenstorrentAI chips; Jim Keller, who designed processors at AMD, Apple and Tesla, joined as CTO in 2020Keller became CEO in 2023: a sign of how much one proven chip architect is worth to a young company

Analysis: the chips were often good. What decided outcomes was usually software, customers and money, which is why the founders who succeed tend to think about those first.

Choosing where to start, by requirement

Nobody starts by taping out a rival to Nvidia’s best chip. There are smaller first steps, and which one suits you depends on what is true for you:

If this is true for youIt leans towards
You want to learn and prove an idea cheaplyAn FPGA: real hardware, reprogrammable, no factory needed
You want your own design in real silicon, as a first stepTiny Tapeout: a small tile on a shared chip, built on the open-source SkyWater 130 nm process; a tile holds about 1,000 logic gates
You need a working prototype chipA multi-project wafer service, where several designs share the cost of masks and wafers
Your strength is architecture, not manufacturingSelling your design as licensable IP, as Imagination Technologies does: its business is designing PowerVR GPUs that other companies build into their chips
Your strength is softwareCompilers, kernels and tools for existing chips, the layer every new chip needs most
You have a narrow workload that GPUs serve badlyA specialised accelerator for that one job, rather than a general-purpose GPU

What an electronics engineer needs to learn

If you studied electronics, here is what you bring and what to add. Analysis, drawn from the roles and bottlenecks above:

You probably haveAdd next
Circuits, transistors, digital logicA hardware description language (Verilog or SystemVerilog) and simulation
Microprocessors and microcontrollersComputer architecture at depth: pipelines, caches, memory bandwidth, and why GPUs run thousands of threads at once (see From Binary to GPUs)
Signals and systems, mathsThe maths of AI workloads: matrix multiplication and how neural networks use it (see Neural Networks)
Some C programmingParallel programming with CUDA or Triton, so you understand what customers will run on your chip
Lab work and testingVerification: test benches and coverage, where most design effort goes
Engineering judgementThe business side: who buys accelerators, what they pay for, and why software decides sales (see How Companies Are Valued)

Who makes what, and where

It is often said that chips are “made in Asia”, and for manufacturing that is largely true. But the chain has many links, and different countries lead different ones:

CountryWhat it leads inA number to know
TaiwanMaking the most advanced chips, for others (TSMC)TSMC produces over 50% of the world’s advanced semiconductors
South KoreaMemory (Samsung, SK Hynix)60.5% of the global memory market and 70.5% of DRAM in 2022
ChinaBuying chips, and a fast-growing industry of its own (SMIC, Hua Hong, JCET)The largest chip market by consumption: 53.7% of worldwide chip sales in 2020, much of it imported
JapanManufacturing equipment (Tokyo Electron); it led memory in the 1980sIn 2007 Taiwan’s chip industry passed America’s, ranking second only to Japan’s
SingaporeAssembly and test since 1968, wafer fabs since 1984; mostly lower-end chipsTexas Instruments and National Semiconductor opened assembly and test plants there in 1968
NetherlandsLithography machines (ASML)The world’s only maker of EUV machines
United StatesChip design, design software (EDA) and equipmentIts share of global fab capacity fell from 37% in 1990 to 10% in 2022; it is projected to rise to 14% by 2032
IndiaDesign talent, and new fabs and packaging under the Semicon India Programme₹76,000 crore, then ₹1,27,500 crore for Semicon 2.0

Analysis: two things follow for anyone outside East Asia. Manufacturing jobs cluster where the fabs are, and new fabs are being built in the US, Europe, Japan and India with government money, which is why technician roles are opening in new places. Design, verification and chip software are not tied to a fab: fabless companies such as Nvidia design chips without owning a factory.

If AI designs chips, how do people get in?

This is the natural worry: why learn chip design if AI will do it? Analysis, from the roles and evidence above, of where people stay valuable even as AI does more:

  • Deciding what to build. A chip is a bet on a workload, a market and a price. AI can explore designs, but someone has to set the goal and the trade-offs.
  • Checking the AI’s work. Verification, proving a design does what is intended, already takes up to 70% of design and development time on large projects; AI-generated designs make it more important, not less. The AlphaChip dispute shows how hard it is to prove that an automated result is really better.
  • Physical work. Running fabs, testing wafers and bringing up new silicon in a lab are hands-on jobs; 39% of the US jobs at risk of going unfilled are technicians.
  • Building the AI tools. Companies such as Synopsys are putting AI into design software, and that needs people who understand both chips and machine learning.

A way in, step by step, using free tools:

  1. Learn binary, gates and the adder on From Binary to GPUs.
  2. Write Verilog and simulate it with free, open-source simulators such as Icarus Verilog or Verilator, which turns Verilog into fast C++ models.
  3. Put the same design on an inexpensive FPGA board, so it runs on real hardware.
  4. Join a Tiny Tapeout workshop and design a tile that is manufactured on a shared chip.
  5. Use an AI assistant to draft Verilog, then prove it with your own test bench; that habit is the job in miniature.
  6. Apply for verification, FPGA or GPU-kernel roles, or train as a technician near a new fab.

Our view: AI will design more and more of each chip, and the people who thrive will be those who can tell it what to build and prove that what it built is right. The other view: automation may shrink the number of entry-level design roles, so the safest entry points may be the physical and verification jobs that AI touches least.

Our view, and the other view

Our view: chips are one of the best bets an engineer can make in the AI era, and an electronics background is a real advantage, not a relic. Start small, with an FPGA or a Tiny Tapeout tile, and grow towards the software that every chip needs.

The other view: the industry is cyclical and capital-hungry, the big prizes are concentrated in a few companies, and AI tools may shrink some design roles. Learning chips is valuable, but building a GPU company is one of the hardest things in technology; most attempts so far have ended in sale or licensing rather than independence.
