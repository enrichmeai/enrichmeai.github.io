Perseverance and resilience

Does the new generation really give up sooner? This page looks at what the evidence says and what it does not, why the causes lie in how childhood and attention have changed rather than in young people themselves, and the habits, practices and relationships that build resilience at any age.

The short answer

If you have ever caught yourself thinking that young people today give up too easily, you are in very old company. Older generations have long described the young as softer, and, as you will see below, research suggests the complaint says more about the people making it than about the young.

What has changed, and is still argued over, is the world young people grow up in: less unsupervised play and risk, phones that compete for every spare minute, and more adults stepping in before small failures happen. Those conditions shape how much practice anyone gets at sticking with something hard. That is why it is not their fault, and also why there is real hope in it: perseverance and resilience are built by experiences, and experiences can be chosen.

First, be careful with the claim

Before looking for causes, it is worth asking whether the thing we are explaining is real. Here is what the research actually found.

  • The complaint is very old. Sociology even has a name for the “inaccurate, exaggerated and sensational characterization of young people”: ephebiphobia. Each older generation tends to compare today’s young with a flattering memory of itself.
  • Researchers have tested the complaint itself. In five preregistered studies with 3,458 American adults, John Protzko and Jonathan Schooler (2019) found that people believe today’s youth are in decline mainly on the traits where they themselves excel: well-read people think young people read less; intelligent people think they are less intelligent. Two mechanisms drive it: we notice other people’s limits where we are strong, and a memory bias projects our present selves onto the young people of the past. The authors suggest this “may explain why the kids these days effect has been happening for millennia”.
  • On self-control, children have got better, not worse. Stephanie Carlson and colleagues, including Walter Mischel, compared 840 children aged 3 to 5 from similar backgrounds who were given the marshmallow task in the late 1960s, the 1980s and the 2000s. The adults they surveyed expected today’s children to wait less. In fact children in the 2000s waited on average about 2 minutes longer than children in the 1960s (out of 10). The authors are careful to say that more research in diverse populations is needed.
  • There is some evidence of strain. Wikipedia’s article on Generation Z reports that when the COVID-19 pandemic struck, the generation proved less resilient than older cohorts, while its values did not change and it stayed open to change, such as hybrid school and remote work.
  • The famous willpower test is weaker than its reputation. In Walter Mischel’s original marshmallow studies from the late 1960s, children could take one treat now or wait for more. A replication with a far larger and more diverse sample found only half the original effect, and suggested that economic background, rather than willpower, explained the other half. Analysis: waiting is easier for a child who has learned that promised rewards really arrive, so what looks like a trait is partly a lesson from the environment.
  • “Grit” is close to an old trait. Angela Duckworth’s grit, which combines perseverance of effort with consistency of interest, correlates strongly with conscientiousness, one of the Big Five personality traits; one large twin study found a genetic correlation of 0.86 between the two. A later meta-analysis concluded that grit’s usefulness may come mainly from its perseverance part.

Analysis: so the honest statement is much narrower than “young people lack perseverance”. Some measures of strain have risen, the causes are argued over, and the qualities involved are shaped by circumstances at least as much as by character.

Why it is not their fault

If something has changed, it is the setting young people grow up in. Each of these explanations has serious supporters and serious critics, so they are shown side by side.

What changedThe argument, and the counter-argument
Childhood moved onto phonesJonathan Haidt’s The Anxious Generation (2024) argues that smartphones, social media and overprotective parenting “rewired” childhood from the late 2000s and caused a rise in mental illness. Developmental psychologist Candice Odgers replied in Nature that most studies have not found a large or consistently negative effect of social media on mental health, and that the link might run the other way, with struggling teenagers using phones more. Her summary: “there are no simple answers”.
Less risk, less unsupervised playHaidt, drawing on the idea of “antifragility”, argues that children need some adversity and challenge to handle difficulty as adults, and that “safetyism” has taken it away. Psychologist Peter Gray’s Free to Learn argues that unleashing the instinct to play makes children happier and more self-reliant. Others argue that home life, poverty and abuse matter far more than screens.
Attention became a productHerbert Simon observed that “a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention”. Apps now compete for that attention by design, which makes boredom, the space where people practise sticking with something, rarer (analysis).
Failure became more visible and less frequentMartin Seligman’s research on learned helplessness, begun in 1967, showed how experiences of having no control teach animals and people to stop trying. Analysis: the reverse holds too. People learn persistence from repeated, survivable struggles that end in success, and a childhood with fewer small failures offers fewer of those lessons.

How resilience is built

Here is the hopeful part. Resilience research began with studies such as Emmy Werner’s forty years of following children from poor backgrounds on Kauai, Hawaii. About a third of the at-risk children grew into well-adjusted adults, which she put down to protective factors in their personalities, their families and their communities. Since then, a handful of levers have gathered good evidence, and none of them depends on the year you were born.

LeverWhat the research saysIn practice
Mastery experiencesAlbert Bandura found that the most effective way to build self-efficacy, the belief that you can succeed at a task, is a personal experience of success. People with high self-efficacy treat hard tasks as things to master, not to avoidTake on problems slightly beyond your level and finish them; keep a list of the hard things you have done
Seeing someone like you succeedBandura’s vicarious experience: watching someone you see as similar to you succeed at something difficult raises your belief that you can do it tooPair with people a year or two ahead of you, not only with experts
Desirable difficultiesRobert Bjork (1994): learning that feels harder, such as spacing practice out and testing yourself instead of re-reading, builds stronger memoryTreat the struggle of learning as a sign it is working, not as failure
Practice under stressDonald Meichenbaum’s stress inoculation training builds resistance to stress through skills training and exposure to stressful situations, in dosesRehearse the hard thing (the demo, the on-call incident, the interview) before it counts
RelationshipsProtective factors in families and communities recur throughout resilience researchA mentor, a team that helps, friends outside work
Believing ability can growCarol Dweck’s growth mindset: people who believe intelligence can be developed tend to embrace challenges and persevere. Critics such as Moreau and colleagues (2019) warn that overemphasising how changeable abilities are can do harmNotice and praise effort and strategy, without pretending effort alone guarantees results

For engineers starting out, and for the people who lead them

Turning that research into a working week looks something like this (analysis, built from the levers above):

If you areTry this
Starting outPick one hard problem a week and stay with it for an hour before asking a person or an AI for the answer. Turn off notifications while you do, and write down what you tried. The habit, not the talent, is what grows
Learning a new fieldSpace your practice over days, test yourself, and build one small thing end to end. One finished project is a mastery experience; ten half-watched tutorials are not
Leading or mentoringGive stretch work with a safety net: a real problem, time to struggle, and help that arrives after the struggle, not before it. Share your own failures, so that struggling looks normal
A parentLeave room for unsupervised play, boredom and small, recoverable failures, and keep your promises, so that waiting is worth it

Our view, and the other view

Our view: the young are not weaker. They have had fewer chances to practise being uncomfortable and coming through it, in a world built to remove discomfort and capture attention. That is not their fault, and the remedy is not lectures but experiences: small, real struggles that end in success, with people around who have been through the same.

The other view: generational comparisons are mostly unreliable, the evidence on phones and mental health is contested, and many young people show great resilience, in a pandemic and in a hard job market. On this view the useful question is not “what is wrong with this generation?” but “what helps anyone, at any age, keep going?”

About the evidence

Psychology has been through a replication crisis, and several famous findings on willpower and mindset turned out smaller than first reported, as the marshmallow replication shows. This page states the claims that survived, names the debates where they are still live, and labels the practical advice as analysis, so you can tell which is which.
