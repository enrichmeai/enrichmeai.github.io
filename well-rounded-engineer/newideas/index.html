Guide / Thinking & character / New Ideas, Familiarity & Habits

New ideas, familiarity and habits

Zero to one and one to n; why a genuinely new idea so often looks crazy and leaves people cold, while the familiar feels right; and how much of what we do runs on habit, so that the habits we choose end up doing much of our thinking for us.

The short answer

Think about the last time someone showed you an idea that was truly new to you. Chances are your first reaction was not delight but a small, polite doubt. That reaction is not a sign that you are closed-minded. It is how human brains are built: we like what we have seen before, we weigh losses more heavily than gains, and we prefer to keep things as they are.

None of this is stupidity. Most of what we do runs automatically, and that is how anyone gets through a busy day. But it has two practical consequences. A new idea has to become familiar before it can feel right, so whoever brings it needs patience as well as evidence. And a good habit, once it has formed, keeps working for you long after the motivation that started it has faded.

Zero to one, and one to n

Zero to One (2014) is Peter Thiel’s book with Blake Masters, based on Masters’ notes from the startup class Thiel taught at Stanford in spring 2012. Its central distinction is a simple one. Going from 0 to 1 means creating something entirely new. Going from 1 to n means copying or extending something that already works. Thiel argues that real progress depends on the first, and he stresses finding “secrets”, things others have not yet seen, and building something unique rather than improving on what the crowd is doing.

KindWhat it isWhat it needs
0 → 1Something that did not exist before: a new kind of product, a new method, a new marketConviction before there is evidence, a thick skin for being called wrong, and a way to make the idea understandable
1 → nTaking something that works to more people, places or scale: the same product in a new market, the same system for ten times the loadDiscipline, repeatability and operations: most of engineering, most of the time

Thiel’s view, and the other view: Thiel puts 0 → 1 first. The other way of seeing it is that most of the benefit of any invention arrives through 1 → n. The diffusion research further down shows that adoption, not invention, is usually the slow part: in a 1943 study, hybrid corn took more than ten years to spread among farmers. And incremental improvement is how most products actually get good. Both kinds of work matter; they simply need different people, or the same people in different modes.

Why new ideas look crazy

Four well-studied effects work against anything new. Each is small on its own; together they explain a lot of polite indifference.

EffectWhat the research found
Mere exposurePeople come to like things simply because they are familiar with them. The effect has been shown with words, Chinese characters, paintings, faces, shapes and sounds. Robert Zajonc, who developed the idea in the 1960s, observed that a new stimulus first brings fear or avoidance; each further exposure brings less fear and more interest, until the once-novel thing is liked.
Processing fluencyThe ease with which the brain processes information. What is easy to take in, because it is clear or has been seen before, tends to feel better than what takes effort.
Status quo biasGiven the same choices, people tend to keep whatever is framed as the current state (Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988). It is linked to loss aversion: losses tend to be treated as if they were about twice as large as an equal gain, so giving up the known feels worse than the new feels good.
The Semmelweis reflexThe reflex-like tendency to reject new evidence because it contradicts established beliefs. It is named after Ignaz Semmelweis, who in 1847 introduced handwashing with chlorinated lime at Vienna General Hospital’s first obstetric clinic. Maternal deaths fell from 18% to under 2%, yet his ideas were rejected; some doctors were offended at being told to wash their hands. They were accepted only after his death, once Louis Pasteur’s germ theory explained them and Joseph Lister acted on it.

Analysis: put those together and a new idea starts with three costs. It is unfamiliar, so it feels wrong. It is hard to process, so it feels doubtful. And it asks people to give up something they already have, which feels like a loss twice its real size. Semmelweis had the evidence and still lost, partly because he had no explanation people could fit to what they already believed. Evidence opens the door; an explanation is what lets people walk through it.

How new ideas spread anyway

And yet new ideas do win, over and over. Everett Rogers’ Diffusion of Innovations (1962) describes how: people adopt in waves, from innovators and early adopters to the early majority, the late majority and finally the laggards. Geoffrey Moore’s Crossing the Chasm (1991) adds that high-tech products face a gap between the early users and the mainstream.

Rogers also lists five things people weigh before adopting something new. They read almost like a checklist for anyone proposing a new tool or design at work:

What people weighRogers’ questionWhen you propose a new tool or design (analysis)
AdvantageIn what way is it better than the alternatives?Measure it on their problem, not yours
CompatibilityHow well does it fit existing values, habits and tools?Show it working alongside what they already use
SimplicityThe easier it is to grasp, the faster it spreadsOne page, one example, one command
ObservabilityCan its benefits be seen, by watching someone else use it?Demo it; let one team succeed in public
TrialabilityCan you try it before committing?A pilot that is easy to undo

Notice how each of these makes the new thing a little more familiar and a little less of a loss, which is exactly what the effects in the previous card demand.

The brain on autopilot

Daniel Kahneman’s Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011) describes two modes of thought. System 1 is fast, automatic and unconscious; System 2 is slow, effortful and logical. (Some priming studies the book cites have been questioned in psychology’s replication crisis; the two-system distinction itself is still widely used.) The brain uses up to about 20% of the body’s energy, more than any other organ, and running on the cheaper, automatic mode most of the time is how it copes (analysis).

Habits are that automatic mode applied to behaviour. William James wrote in 1890 that “any sequence of mental action which has been frequently repeated tends to perpetuate itself”, so that we act “without any consciously formed purpose”. Research since then describes a habit as behaviour that, repeated in a consistent context, becomes automatic: efficient, needing little awareness, triggered by the context itself. The basal ganglia appear to remember the context that triggers a habit, and can revive it when the trigger returns. Donald Hebb’s 1949 theory is often summed up as “neurons that fire together, wire together”.

How long it takes. In a study by Phillippa Lally and colleagues (2009), people took on average 66 days for a new daily behaviour to become as automatic as it would get, with a range of 18 to 254 days. So it is not 21 days, and it is not the same for everyone. If a new habit still feels like effort after three weeks, nothing has gone wrong.

“The brain is brainless; our habits make it better”

Our view: most of what the brain does, it does without thinking: it repeats what it has repeated before. In that sense it is brainless, and that is not an insult but a design. Automatic behaviour is what frees attention for the few things that really need it. It follows that the habits you choose decide much of what you do on any ordinary day. So choose them on purpose: a small daily practice, in the same place and at the same time, kept up for two months or more, will run on its own long after motivation has faded. The same mechanism explains the rest of this page. We like the familiar because the familiar is what our habits already handle, so a new idea feels crazy until it has been seen often enough to become one of them.

The other view: calling the brain brainless undersells the automatic mode. Fast, intuitive judgement is also where expertise lives: an experienced engineer’s sense that a design is wrong is System 1, trained by years of practice. And habits cut both ways. The same automaticity that keeps a good routine going keeps a bad one going, and it is what makes us dismiss the next Semmelweis. Habits make the brain better only when we keep checking which ones we are building.

Putting it to work

Here is how the research above translates into everyday choices (analysis):

If you want toDo this
Get a new idea acceptedMake it familiar before you ask for a decision: show it more than once, connect it to what people already know, give an explanation as well as evidence, and offer a small trial that is easy to undo
Judge a new idea fairlyNotice your first reaction (“that’s crazy”) as unfamiliarity rather than evidence, and ask what would have to be true for it to work
Build a habitKeep it small, tie it to the same cue (a time, a place, something you already do), repeat it daily, and expect about two months, not three weeks
Break a habitChange the context that triggers it, because the trigger, not the intention, is what starts the routine
