How companies are valued

Why can a company that loses money today be worth billions, and why does its share price lurch on news that barely changes this year’s results? This page walks through how investors value a business on the cash it may produce over the next ten years and beyond, with a worked example you can run yourself, the shortcuts people use instead, and the parts of the sum that engineers quietly change every day.

The short answer

Strip away the jargon and a company is worth one thing: the cash it will hand to its owners from now on, counted in today’s money. The method behind that sentence is called discounted cash flow, or DCF. It values a security, a project, a company or any other asset by building in the time value of money, the simple fact that money later is worth less than money now. Irving Fisher (The Theory of Interest, 1930) and John Burr Williams (The Theory of Investment Value, 1938) were the first to set it out in modern economic terms.

So when someone says a company is valued on what it will be worth in ten years, they mean three steps. First, forecast the cash it will make for about ten years. Second, estimate what everything after that is worth. Third, bring both back to today’s money. A share price is simply the market’s running answer to those three questions, which is why it moves whenever the answers change, even if nothing has happened yet.

Two phrases you will hear: the market value is what buyers are paying today; the intrinsic value is what a model says the cash is worth. Wikipedia points out that intrinsic value is subject to opinion and varies between analysts. When an analyst says “buy”, they are saying the second is higher than the first.

Step 1: money later is worth less than money now

Imagine being offered £1,000 today or £1,000 in ten years. You would take it today, and not just out of impatience: money you have now can be invested and grow, so a pound today is worth more than a pound next year. To compare amounts at different times, you discount future money back to today:

value today = amount ÷ (1 + r)years, where r is the return you could have earned instead.

At 10% a year, that £1,000 in ten years is worth about £386 today (1,000 ÷ 1.110). The further away the money, and the higher the rate, the less it counts now. Hold on to that one line: everything else on this page is built from it.

Step 2: the discount rate is the price of risk

Which rate should you use? For a company it is usually the weighted average cost of capital (WACC): the average return the business is expected to pay everyone who funds it, lenders and shareholders alike, weighted by how much each puts in. Think of it as the minimum return the business has to earn, because if it earns less, the people funding it “will invest elsewhere”. Notably, it is set by the market, not by management. The shareholders’ part is often estimated with the capital asset pricing model, which asks for a higher return from riskier shares.

Analysis: this is why the very same forecast is worth less for a risky start-up than for a utility, and why rising interest rates hit hardest the companies whose cash lies mostly in the future. A higher rate shrinks money that is far away much more than money that is close.

Step 3: forecast ten years, then value everything after

Nobody can forecast a business year by year for decades; beyond a few years, the uncertainty about the industry and the economy swamps any spreadsheet. So a DCF forecasts a fixed stretch, often five to ten years, and then adds one figure for everything after it: the terminal value. There are two common ways to estimate it, and it helps to know both, because they fail in different ways.

MethodHow it worksWatch for
Perpetuity growthAssume the cash keeps growing at a steady rate g for ever: terminal value = next year’s cash ÷ (discount rate − g). This is the Gordon growth model, published by Myron Gordon and Eli Shapiro in 1956, building on Williams’ 1938 book.Both the discount rate and g are guesses, and the answer hangs on the small gap between them
Exit multipleAssume the business is sold at the end of the forecast, at a multiple of its earnings seen in comparable sales, often enterprise value to EBITDAMultiples change over time; today’s may be high or low by historical standards

Whichever you choose, the terminal value is then discounted back to today, just like each forecast year, and the two are added together.

Run it: a company valued on its next ten years

Let’s make it concrete with a made-up company. It produces £10 million of free cash this year, and we expect that to grow by 20% a year for ten years, then by 3% a year for ever. Investors want a 10% return. The script below is plain Python; save it as dcf.py and run python3 dcf.py.

"""Value a company from the cash it may produce over 10 years, plus everything after."""

def value(cash_now, growth, years, discount, terminal_growth):
    total, cash = 0.0, cash_now
    for year in range(1, years + 1):
        cash *= 1 + growth                              # this year's free cash flow
        total += cash / (1 + discount) ** year          # what it is worth today
    after = cash * (1 + terminal_growth) / (discount - terminal_growth)   # all later years, at year 10
    after_today = after / (1 + discount) ** years
    return total + after_today, after_today

worth, from_after = value(cash_now=10, growth=0.20, years=10, discount=0.10, terminal_growth=0.03)
print(f"value today: {worth:.0f} million; share from after year 10: {from_after / worth:.0%}")

for d in (0.09, 0.10, 0.11):
    print(f"discount {d:.0%}:", "  ".join(f"g={g:.0%} -> {value(10, 0.20, 10, d, g)[0]:.0f}" for g in (0.02, 0.03, 0.04)))

# reverse: what 10-year growth does a price of 600 million assume?
low, high = 0.0, 1.0
for _ in range(60):
    mid = (low + high) / 2
    low, high = (mid, high) if value(10, mid, 10, 0.10, 0.03)[0] < 600 else (low, mid)
print(f"a price of 600 million assumes about {mid:.0%} growth a year for 10 years")

Here is what it printed when we ran it:

value today: 518 million; share from after year 10: 68%
discount 9%: g=2% -> 557  g=3% -> 625  g=4% -> 720
discount 10%: g=2% -> 471  g=3% -> 518  g=4% -> 580
discount 11%: g=2% -> 405  g=3% -> 438  g=4% -> 481
a price of 600 million assumes about 22% growth a year for 10 years

Three things are worth pausing on.

  • Most of the value lies beyond the forecast. Of the £518 million, 68% comes from cash after year ten. The ten years we can actually picture are worth £167 million; everything after them is worth £351 million. Analysis: for a fast-growing company this share is high, which is why the long-run story matters more to its price than next quarter.
  • Small changes in the inputs move the answer a lot. One point off the discount rate (9% instead of 10%) adds about a fifth to the value; one point more of long-run growth adds about an eighth. Two analysts with honest, slightly different assumptions can easily disagree by half.
  • You can run it backwards. Instead of asking what the company is worth, ask what a price assumes. A price of £600 million only makes sense if the cash grows about 22% a year for ten years. That turns a vague feeling into a question you can argue about with evidence: has any company in this market ever grown that fast for that long?

The shortcuts: multiples

Most people never build a DCF. Wikipedia groups valuation into three approaches: discounted cash flow, relative valuation (pricing a company against similar ones), and contingent claim valuation (pricing options). Relative valuation is where the ratios you hear on the news come from. They are quicker than a DCF, but they hide the same assumptions inside a single number.

RatioWhat it isWhat it hides
P/EShare price divided by earnings per shareA high P/E is the market paying for growth it expects; with no earnings, it means nothing
PEGP/E divided by annual growth in earnings per shareWikipedia calls it “only a rough rule of thumb”
EV/EBITDAEnterprise value (the claims of all lenders and shareholders, so more complete than market capitalisation) divided by earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisationLeaves out the cost of replacing equipment and servers
Rule of 40For software-as-a-service companies: revenue growth (%) plus profit margin (%) should be at least 40%A heuristic, and the 40% is, in Wikipedia’s words, an arbitrary benchmark

Analysis: every multiple is a DCF with the working hidden. When one company trades at a P/E of 40 and another at 10, the market is saying it expects much more growth, or much less risk, from the first. A multiple tells you what the market believes; a DCF lets you check whether you believe it too.

Why prices move so much when so little changes

Put the chart above next to a share price and the volatility starts to make sense. If most of a company’s value lies ten or more years away, its price depends on beliefs about a future nobody can see, and when those beliefs shift, the price shifts with them, even if this year’s results hardly move. Yahoo is the example on this site: in 2008 it turned down an offer of $44.6 billion from Microsoft, and in 2017 Verizon bought its internet business for $4.48 billion (see Case Studies). The same arithmetic explains the opposite puzzle too: a company can be worth a great deal while it still makes a loss, because the value sits in the later years, for as long as investors believe those years will come.

What engineers change in the numbers

It is tempting to think valuation is something that happens in finance, far from the code. But look at the inputs again, and you will see that an engineering team touches every one of them (analysis):

InputWhat moves it
GrowthHow quickly features reach customers, how reliable the product is, whether it can scale without a rewrite (see Idea to Production)
MarginWhat it costs to serve each customer: cloud spend, support load, manual work (see Cloud Cost)
Risk, and so the discount rateOutages, breaches and fragile systems. Yahoo’s breaches took $350 million off its sale price
How long the advantage lasts, and so the terminal valueWhether what you build is hard to copy, and whether the system can keep changing for another ten years

Our view, and the other view

Our view: learn to run a DCF backwards. You may never value a company yourself, but asking “what does this price assume?” turns a mood (“it’s overvalued”) into a claim you can check, whether it is about your employer, a start-up offer paid partly in shares, or a company in the news.

The other view: a DCF is only as good as its guesses, and most of its answer comes from the least knowable part, the terminal value. That is why many investors lean on multiples, comparable deals and judgement about the business itself, and treat a precise DCF figure as false confidence.

Choose by requirement

If this is true for youLean towards
The business has steady, predictable cashA DCF with perpetuity growth
It makes no profit yetA DCF over scenarios (it works, it half works, it fails), or revenue multiples of similar companies
You want a quick comparison with its peersMultiples: P/E, EV/EBITDA, or the Rule of 40 for software
The company is being bought or soldExit multiples from comparable acquisitions, checked against a DCF
