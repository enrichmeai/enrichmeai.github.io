How companies are valued

Why a company that makes a loss today can be worth billions, and why its price can swing on news that barely changes this year’s results: valuing a business on the cash it may produce over the next ten years and beyond, with a worked example you can run, the shortcuts people use instead, and what engineers change in the numbers.

The short answer

A company is worth the cash it will produce for its owners from now on, counted in today’s money. The standard method is discounted cash flow (DCF): a way to value a security, project, company or asset that builds in the time value of money. Irving Fisher (The Theory of Interest, 1930) and John Burr Williams (The Theory of Investment Value, 1938) first set it out in modern economic terms.

So when people say a company is valued on what it will be worth in ten years, they mean three steps: forecast its cash for about ten years, estimate the value of everything after that, and convert both into today’s money. The share price is the market’s running answer to those three questions, and it moves whenever the answers change.

The market value is what buyers pay today. The intrinsic value is what a model says the cash is worth; Wikipedia notes it is subject to opinion and varies between analysts. A “buy” recommendation is an analyst saying the second is higher than the first.

Step 1: money later is worth less than money now

Money you have today can be invested and grow, so a pound today is worth more than a pound next year. To compare money at different times, you discount future amounts back to today: value today = amount ÷ (1 + r)years, where r is the return you could have earned instead.

At 10% a year, £1,000 received in ten years is worth about £386 today (1,000 ÷ 1.110). The further away the cash, and the higher the rate, the less it counts now. Everything else on this page follows from that one line.

Step 2: the discount rate is the price of risk

The rate used for a company is usually its weighted average cost of capital (WACC): the average return the company is expected to pay everyone who funds it, lenders and shareholders, weighted by how much each provides. It is the minimum return the business must earn “or they will invest elsewhere”, and it is set by the market, not by management. The shareholders’ part is often estimated with the capital asset pricing model, which charges more for riskier shares.

Analysis: this is why the same forecast is worth less for a risky start-up than for a utility, and why rising interest rates pull down the value of companies whose cash is mostly far in the future: a higher rate shrinks distant cash much more than near cash.

Step 3: forecast ten years, then value everything after

Forecasting a business year by year for decades is impractical; beyond a few years the uncertainty about the industry and the economy is too great. So a DCF forecasts a fixed period, often five to ten years, and adds a terminal value: the value, at the end of the forecast, of all the cash after it. There are two ways to estimate it:

MethodHow it worksWatch for
Perpetuity growthAssume the cash keeps growing at a steady rate g for ever: terminal value = next year’s cash ÷ (discount rate − g). This is the Gordon growth model, published by Myron Gordon and Eli Shapiro in 1956, building on Williams’ 1938 book.Both the discount rate and g are guesses, and the result depends on the small gap between them
Exit multipleAssume the business is sold at the end of the forecast, at a multiple of its earnings seen in comparable sales, often enterprise value to EBITDAMultiples change over time; today’s may be high or low by historical standards

Either way, the terminal value is then discounted back to today and added to the value of the forecast years.

Run it: a company valued on its next ten years

A made-up company produces £10 million of free cash this year and is expected to grow it 20% a year for ten years, then 3% a year for ever. Investors want 10% a year. The script is plain Python; save it as dcf.py and run python3 dcf.py.

"""Value a company from the cash it may produce over 10 years, plus everything after."""

def value(cash_now, growth, years, discount, terminal_growth):
    total, cash = 0.0, cash_now
    for year in range(1, years + 1):
        cash *= 1 + growth                              # this year's free cash flow
        total += cash / (1 + discount) ** year          # what it is worth today
    after = cash * (1 + terminal_growth) / (discount - terminal_growth)   # all later years, at year 10
    after_today = after / (1 + discount) ** years
    return total + after_today, after_today

worth, from_after = value(cash_now=10, growth=0.20, years=10, discount=0.10, terminal_growth=0.03)
print(f"value today: {worth:.0f} million; share from after year 10: {from_after / worth:.0%}")

for d in (0.09, 0.10, 0.11):
    print(f"discount {d:.0%}:", "  ".join(f"g={g:.0%} -> {value(10, 0.20, 10, d, g)[0]:.0f}" for g in (0.02, 0.03, 0.04)))

# reverse: what 10-year growth does a price of 600 million assume?
low, high = 0.0, 1.0
for _ in range(60):
    mid = (low + high) / 2
    low, high = (mid, high) if value(10, mid, 10, 0.10, 0.03)[0] < 600 else (low, mid)
print(f"a price of 600 million assumes about {mid:.0%} growth a year for 10 years")

What it printed when we ran it:

value today: 518 million; share from after year 10: 68%
discount 9%: g=2% -> 557  g=3% -> 625  g=4% -> 720
discount 10%: g=2% -> 471  g=3% -> 518  g=4% -> 580
discount 11%: g=2% -> 405  g=3% -> 438  g=4% -> 481
a price of 600 million assumes about 22% growth a year for 10 years

Three things to notice.

  • Most of the value is beyond the forecast. 68% of the £518 million comes from cash after year ten. Analysis: for a fast-growing company this share is high, which is why the long-run story matters more than next quarter.
  • Small changes in assumptions move the answer a lot. One point on the discount rate (9% instead of 10%) adds about a fifth; one point on long-run growth adds about an eighth. Two analysts with honest, slightly different inputs can disagree by half.
  • You can run it backwards. Instead of asking what the company is worth, ask what a price assumes. A price of £600 million only makes sense if the cash grows about 22% a year for ten years. That is a question you can argue about with evidence: has any company in this market grown that fast for that long?

The shortcuts: multiples

Wikipedia groups valuation into three approaches: discounted cash flow, relative valuation (pricing a company against similar ones), and contingent claim valuation (pricing options). Relative valuation uses ratios, which are quicker than a DCF and hide the same assumptions inside one number.

RatioWhat it isWhat it hides
P/EShare price divided by earnings per shareA high P/E is the market paying for growth it expects; it is meaningless when there are no earnings
PEGP/E divided by annual growth in earnings per shareWikipedia calls it “only a rough rule of thumb”
EV/EBITDAEnterprise value (the claims of all lenders and shareholders, so more complete than market capitalisation) divided by earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisationIgnores the cost of replacing equipment and servers
Rule of 40For software-as-a-service companies: revenue growth (%) plus profit margin (%) should be at least 40%A heuristic, and the 40% is, in Wikipedia’s words, an arbitrary benchmark

Analysis: every multiple is a DCF with the working hidden. A P/E of 40 on a company and 10 on another is the market saying it expects much more growth, or much less risk, from the first. A multiple tells you what the market believes; a DCF lets you check whether you believe it too.

Why prices move so much when so little changes

If most of a company’s value lies ten or more years away, its price depends on beliefs about a future nobody can see. When those beliefs change, the price changes a lot, even if this year’s results hardly move. Yahoo is the example on this site: in 2008 it rejected an offer of $44.6 billion from Microsoft, and in 2017 Verizon bought its internet business for $4.48 billion (see Case Studies). The same model also explains why a company can be worth a lot while it still makes a loss: the value is in the later years, as long as investors believe those years will come.

What engineers change in the numbers

Every input in the model is something an engineering team affects (analysis):

InputWhat moves it
GrowthHow fast features reach customers, how reliable the product is, whether it can scale without a rewrite (see Idea to Production)
MarginThe cost to serve each customer: cloud spend, support load, manual work (see Cloud Cost)
Risk, so the discount rateOutages, breaches and fragile systems. Yahoo’s breaches took $350 million off its sale price
How long the advantage lasts, so the terminal valueWhether what you build is hard to copy, and whether the system can keep changing for ten years

Our view, and the other view

Our view: learn to run a DCF backwards. You will rarely value a company yourself, but asking “what does this price assume?” turns a mood (“it’s overvalued”) into a claim you can check, about your employer, a start-up offer paid in shares, or a company in the news. The other view: a DCF is only as good as its guesses, and most of its answer comes from the least knowable part (the terminal value). Many investors therefore rely on multiples, comparable deals and judgement about the business, and treat a precise DCF figure as false confidence.

Choose by requirement

If this is true for youLean towards
The business has steady, predictable cashA DCF with perpetuity growth
It makes no profit yetA DCF over scenarios (it works, it half works, it fails), or revenue multiples of similar companies
You want to compare it with its peers quicklyMultiples: P/E, EV/EBITDA, or the Rule of 40 for software
The company is being bought or soldExit multiples from comparable acquisitions, checked against a DCF
You want to judge a price someone else setA reverse DCF: what growth does it assume?
