Philosophy for engineers

Philosophy is the practice of thinking clearly about hard questions: how we know things, how to reason, what matters and what we owe each other. Engineers use it every day without the names. Knowing the names makes it easier to do well.

Epistemology: how do you know?

  • Knowledge needs justification: a belief that turns out true by luck is not knowledge. "The tests passed" is justified only if you saw them run.
  • Sources differ in reliability: the running system beats the config, the config beats the docs, the docs beat memory, memory beats rumour.
  • Absence of evidence is evidence of absence only if you looked properly. Say how thoroughly you looked.
  • Calibration: match your confidence to your evidence, and say how sure you are.
  • Testimony, including an AI's answer or a colleague's summary, is a reason to check, not a replacement for checking.
  • Different questions need different methods: experiments settle what is true of the world; ethics, judgement and tradition speak to what we should do and what matters. See what science can answer, and what it cannot settle alone.

Reasoning well

KindShapeEngineering example
DeductionIf the premises are true, the conclusion must be"Every write goes through this function; it validates input; so every write is validated."
InductionGeneralising from observations"It failed on every run with a null date; nulls probably cause it."
AbductionThe best explanation for what you see"Latency rose right after the deploy; the deploy is the most likely cause."

Only deduction is certain, and only if the premises are. Induction and abduction give hypotheses to test, which is where the scientific method takes over.

Fallacies in technical debates

  • Appeal to authority: "Big Company uses it, so it is right for us."
  • Bandwagon: "Everyone is moving to X."
  • False dilemma: "Either we rewrite everything or we live with it."
  • Straw man: arguing against a weaker version of a colleague's proposal.
  • Ad hominem: judging the idea by who proposed it.
  • Post hoc: "It broke after my change, so my change broke it." Maybe, but test it.
  • Appeal to novelty or tradition: new is not better, old is not proven.

Useful razors and principles

  • Occam's razor: prefer the explanation or design with the fewest assumptions.
  • Hanlon's razor: do not assume malice where a mistake explains it. Most broken things are accidents.
  • Chesterton's fence: do not remove something until you know why it was put there.
  • First principles: reason from what is known to be true rather than from analogy and habit.
  • Inversion: ask how this could fail, then prevent that.
  • Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Tests written to raise coverage, not to catch bugs, are an example.

Systems thinking

  • Feedback loops: reinforcing loops amplify (retries cause load, load causes timeouts, timeouts cause retries); balancing loops stabilise (autoscaling).
  • Second-order effects: ask "and then what?" A cache speeds reads, and then serves stale data, and then someone acts on it.
  • Delays in a system cause overshoot: scaling decisions based on old metrics oscillate.
  • The structure produces the behaviour: repeated incidents point to a system to change, not a person to blame.
  • Leverage points: small changes in rules, incentives and information flows often matter more than large changes in parameters.

Why big organisations move slowly

  • Their processes are scar tissue. Most approvals, reviews and controls exist because something went wrong once. Each one made sense; together they slow everything down.
  • More people, many more conversations. Ten people have 45 possible pairs to keep in step; a hundred have 4,950. Agreement gets expensive.
  • Blame is lopsided. A change that fails is noticed and blamed; a chance that was missed is rarely noticed at all. So people learn that "no" is safer than "yes".
  • Today's customers pull hard. Clayton Christensen's The Innovator's Dilemma (1997) showed how successful companies, by doing what their best customers ask, can miss the new thing that replaces them.
  • The system mirrors the organisation. Conway's law (Melvin Conway, 1968): a system's design copies the communication structure of the organisation that built it, so changing one often means changing the other.
  • Some slowness is care. A bank or a hospital that changes something wrongly hurts millions of people. Slow is sometimes the right speed.

What helps: small teams that own their area, decisions made close to the work, and telling reversible decisions (move fast) from one-way doors (take care). Amazon's 2015 shareholder letter calls these "two-way" and "one-way" doors.

Why people find it hard to change

  • Habits save effort. The brain turns what we repeat into habit, so it needs little thought. Changing means thinking hard again about things that used to be easy.
  • Skill becomes identity. After years in one technology or one way of working, "I am the person who knows X" is part of who you are. Change can feel like losing that, and becoming a beginner again.
  • Losses feel bigger than gains. Kahneman and Tversky's research showed people weigh a loss more heavily than an equal gain, so what we might lose looms larger than what we might win.
  • We prefer what we have: status quo bias. And the more we have invested in a path, the harder it is to leave it, even when we should (the sunk-cost trap).
  • Old scars: people who lived through a change that went badly remember it, and they are not wrong to.

What helps: explain why, start with a small experiment, make it safe to be a beginner, let people shape the change, and value the experience they bring to it. The brain keeps learning at any age (see Body, Mind & Nature); change is hard, not impossible. Be patient with others, and honest with yourself about your own habits.

Why every decision has people for it and against it

For every option we choose, some people will find it right and some will find it wrong. Usually that is not because one side is foolish. It is because they stand in different places.

  • They see different things: the person on call sees the risk, the product owner sees the customer waiting, finance sees the cost, the new joiner sees what nobody questions any more.
  • They pay different prices. Every option has a cost, and it falls on someone. A choice that is cheap for one team is extra work, or risk, for another.
  • They value different things: speed or safety, simplicity or flexibility, today or next year. These are trade-offs, not right and wrong answers.
  • So two honest, sensible people can disagree. Assume good faith first.

What to do about it: say the trade-offs out loud, name who pays the cost, and listen hardest to the people who disagree, because they often see the risk you missed. Then decide, write down why (an architecture decision record is one way), and agree when you will look again. Once a decision is made, commit to making it work, even if you argued against it: Amazon calls this "disagree and commit". Aim for decisions people understand, not decisions everyone likes.

Ethics: what we owe users and society

LensAsksIn an engineering decision
ConsequencesWhat outcomes does this cause, for whom?Who is harmed if this model is wrong, and how badly?
Duties and rightsWhat rules and rights apply, regardless of outcome?Do we have consent to use this data? Can users delete it?
VirtueWhat would a person of good character do?Would I be comfortable explaining this design to the people it affects?
FairnessAre burdens and benefits shared justly?Does this work as well for users with disabilities, in other languages, on cheaper phones?
  • Professional codes (ACM, IEEE) put the public good, honesty, and avoiding harm first.
  • AI raises the stakes: automated decisions at scale, opaque reasoning, and content that can mislead. Build in human review where harm is possible.
  • You may be asked to build something wrong. Raise it, explain the harm, propose an alternative, and know where your line is.

Stoic habits for hard days

  • Separate what you control from what you do not: you control your response to an incident, not that it happened.
  • Expect setbacks: plan for failure, so it is a known path, not a shock.
  • Judge actions, not outcomes alone: a good decision can turn out badly; learn from it without self-blame.
  • Daily review: what went well, what to do differently, what you learned.

Socratic questions for reviews and design

  • What exactly do you mean by…?
  • How do we know that is true?
  • What are we assuming?
  • What would change your mind?
  • What is the strongest argument against this?
  • What follows if we are wrong?

Asked with curiosity, these improve the work. Asked to win, they damage trust.

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Ask "how do we know?" before accepting a claim
  • Recognise common fallacies in debates
  • Know the professional codes of ethics exist and what they ask

2 Practitioner

  • Reason clearly with deduction, induction and abduction
  • Use razors and principles (Occam, Chesterton, Goodhart) in decisions
  • Raise ethical concerns about a feature constructively

3 Senior

  • Apply systems thinking to recurring problems and incentives
  • Weigh ethical trade-offs in designs with several lenses
  • Lead discussions with Socratic questions that improve the work

4 Expert

  • Shape organisational values, incentives and ethical standards
  • Advise on AI ethics and societal impact
  • Model wise judgement under uncertainty

Each level includes the ones before it. See Start here for what the levels mean.
