Guide / Thinking & character / Biohacking & Longevity

Biohacking and longevity

Dave Asprey, Bryan Johnson and Sajeev Nair have made “biohacking” famous: treating your own body like a system to measure and tune. What each of them does and believes, what their supporters and critics say, why mainstream medicine has been wrong before too, and why one of the best-supported habits for a long life might simply be not to argue.

The short answer

Engineers are natural biohackers. We like dashboards, experiments and the idea that a system can be measured and improved, so it is no surprise that wearables, supplement stacks and “biological age” scores appeal to us. Some of that curiosity is healthy, and some of it runs ahead of the evidence. Running ahead can mean being wrong, or it can mean being early.

Mainstream medicine has its own record to answer for: it has had to reverse a surprising number of its standard practices, and it once ridiculed ideas that later won a Nobel Prize. So this page tries to give everyone’s view fairly: the biohackers’, their critics’, and the doctors’. Nobody here is fully right, and nobody is fully wrong.

Where almost everyone agrees, biohackers and doctors alike, is on the basics, and they are cheap, unglamorous and available to everyone: move, sleep, don’t smoke, keep alcohol low, and above all keep warm relationships and stay out of hostile ones. This page looks at the famous names first, then at the evidence.

This page is general information, not medical advice. Talk to a doctor before changing medication, diet or exercise in a significant way, and be especially careful with anything sold as a treatment.

Dave Asprey: Bulletproof

Dave Asprey, born in 1973, is a former technology executive (Trend Micro, Blue Coat, Citrix) who did much to popularise the word “biohacker”. He is best known for Bulletproof Coffee and the Bulletproof diet, a low-carbohydrate, high-fat plan; he founded Bulletproof 360 in 2013, later moved into franchised “Upgrade Labs”, and has said in interviews that he plans to live to 180. His books include Smarter Not Harder: The Biohacker’s Guide to Getting the Body and Mind You Want (2023).

His view: you do not have to accept the body you were given; you can measure it, experiment on it and take control of your own biology, without waiting for permission.

Where people disagree: supporters credit him with making self-experimentation popular. Dietitians have criticised some of his claims about the Bulletproof diet as pseudoscientific, and one newspaper review called the diet “simplistic, invalid and unscientific”.

Bryan Johnson: Blueprint and “don’t die”

Bryan Johnson founded the payments company Braintree, which bought Venmo in 2012 and was acquired by PayPal for $800 million in 2013. Since 2021 he has turned his own body into a very public experiment called Blueprint, with a company selling supplements and foods drawn from his protocol. He calls the philosophy “don’t die”, says the aim is to reduce the biological age of his organs to that of an 18-year-old, and is the subject of the 2025 Netflix documentary Don’t Die: The Man Who Wants to Live Forever.

Some of what he does is ordinary good practice, carried out with unusual discipline: sleep, diet, exercise. Some is experimental: in 2023 he had monthly one-litre plasma transfusions for six months, including one from his 17-year-old son. He reports scores on biomarkers such as DunedinPACE, a DNA methylation measure that estimates the pace of ageing.

His view: death should not be accepted as inevitable, and the body can be run by measurement rather than by willpower and habit. Since 2023 he has shared his biomarker data publicly, so others can follow, compare or check it.

Where people disagree: supporters see an unusually open experiment, done in public. Researchers in fields related to ageing, such as McGill pharmacologist Moshe Szyf, have questioned whether science can yet achieve what he is aiming for. In 2026 Johnson announced that he had been diagnosed with an incurable autoimmune disease, a reminder that no protocol controls everything.

Sajeev Nair: biohacking, the Indian way

Sajeev Nair is a Bengaluru-based entrepreneur often described in the Indian press as one of the country’s leading biohackers. He founded Vieroots Wellness Solutions, which combines genetic and gut microbiome testing, data and AI with traditional practices from Ayurveda and yoga, and wrote The Making of a Superhuman. He tests changes on himself and has said that his vegan diet, exercise and experimental procedures have made his biological age five years younger.

His view: modern measurement and India’s traditional practices work better together than apart: test your genes and gut, then use diet, yoga and lifestyle to act on what you find.

Where people disagree: supporters value an approach that builds on Ayurveda and yoga instead of discarding them. The five-year figure is his own claim, not an independent finding, and some coverage of his work appears in promotional features. The Hindu has noted that India has no regulations that directly govern biohacking, so there is not yet an independent regulator checking the claims.

Doctors are not always right either

It would be unfair to hold biohackers to a standard that mainstream medicine does not always meet itself. The record is humbling:

  • Standard practice gets reversed, often. Vinay Prasad and colleagues read ten years of original articles in one leading medical journal (2001 to 2010). Of the 363 that tested an established standard of care, 146, about 40%, found it was no better than a lesser alternative (a “reversal”); 138 reaffirmed it.
  • Good ideas have been ridiculed. In the 1840s Ignaz Semmelweis showed that doctors washing their hands cut deaths from childbed fever; his ideas conflicted with the medical views of the time and were rejected by the medical community.
  • A self-experiment won a Nobel Prize. Barry Marshall and Robin Warren argued that a bacterium, Helicobacter pylori, causes many stomach ulcers, against decades of doctrine blaming stress, spicy food and acid; established doctors ridiculed the idea. Marshall drank a culture of the bacterium to prove it, and the two won the Nobel Prize in 2005.

Analysis: the lesson is not that doctors are wrong or that biohackers are right. It is that everyone, expert or self-experimenter, should be judged by evidence, and should change their mind when the evidence changes. Medicine at its best does exactly that, which is why it reverses itself.

The double standard: ultra-processed food, alcohol and cigarettes

Our view: there is an odd double standard in how people judge health choices. Someone who measures their sleep, cuts out sugar or tries an unusual diet is often mocked as a crank, while ultra-processed food, alcohol and cigarettes are taken without a second thought, because they are normal. Yet the everyday choices have far stronger evidence of harm than most biohacks:

  • Ultra-processed food, factory-made products built from ingredients with no use in a home kitchen, made up 58% of daily calories in the United States and 57% in the United Kingdom in 2023. An umbrella review in the BMJ (Lane and colleagues, 2024), covering 45 pooled analyses and almost ten million people, found greater exposure associated with 32 of the health outcomes studied, with convincing evidence for higher risk of death from cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, anxiety and common mental disorders.
  • Tobacco kills more than 7 million people a year, according to the World Health Organization, making smoking the most common cause of preventable early death.
  • Alcohol: the WHO estimates 3 million deaths a year from harmful use, 5.3% of all deaths worldwide.

Why the familiar gets a free pass. Psychologists have names for it. Status quo bias is a preference for keeping the existing state of affairs; the mere-exposure effect is the tendency to like things simply because they are familiar. A habit everyone shares feels safe; a new one feels risky, whatever the evidence says. The same pattern appears on New Ideas, Familiarity & Habits.

The other view: the scepticism aimed at biohackers is partly earned, because some of them sell the products they recommend, while nobody is selling “eat less processed food”. The evidence on ultra-processed food is also less settled than it can sound: the BMJ review rated most of its pooled analyses as low or very low quality, the studies are mostly observational, the definition itself has been criticised as too vague, and there have been few clinical trials. On that view, the fair rule is to apply the same standard to both: ask for evidence before trying something new, and ask for it about the old habits too.

Fat, sugar and the Standard American Diet

Our view: the modern Western diet was shaped partly by weak or one-sided research. Natural fats were made the villain, sugar escaped blame, and factory-made seed oils spread into almost everything. Some of that history is now well documented:

  • The sugar industry steered the science. Researchers at the University of California, San Francisco, reading the industry’s own internal documents, found that the Sugar Research Foundation sponsored a 1965 review in the New England Journal of Medicine that singled out fat and cholesterol as the dietary causes of heart disease and played down sugar. The foundation set the review’s objective and saw drafts, and its funding was not disclosed (Kearns and colleagues, JAMA Internal Medicine, 2016).
  • A trial that did not support the theory was only partly published. The Minnesota Coronary Experiment (1968–73) replaced saturated fat with vegetable oil rich in linoleic acid. Recovered data, analysed in the BMJ in 2016, showed cholesterol fell, but there was no benefit on deaths; each 30 mg/dL fall in cholesterol came with a 22% higher risk of death. The authors concluded that incomplete publication had led to the benefits of replacing saturated fat with vegetable oils being overestimated.
  • Seed oils really did spread. In the United States, estimated consumption of soybean oil per person rose more than 1,000-fold between 1909 and 1999, and linoleic acid went from about 2.8% to 7.2% of energy (Blasbalg and colleagues, 2011).

The other view: the case against saturated fat did not rest on one trial. A 2020 Cochrane review of 15 randomised trials with about 59,000 people found that cutting saturated fat reduced combined cardiovascular events by 21% (moderate-quality evidence), though with little or no effect on deaths, and that replacing it with polyunsaturated fat or carbohydrate appeared useful. On that view, the sugar scandal and the Minnesota data show how research can be bent, not that fat is harmless or that seed oils are poison.

Analysis: the parts both sides can agree on are the lessons: ask who paid for a study, look for the trials that were never published, and be wary of anyone, an industry or an influencer, who is completely certain about nutrition.

What decades of evidence point to

Alongside the experiments, set the long, patient studies of ordinary lives. Two stand out.

  • The Harvard Study of Adult Development began in 1938 and has followed 268 Harvard-educated men, most from the classes of 1942 to 1944, for more than 85 years; it is now led by Robert Waldinger. Among its findings: alcohol abuse, together with the smoking that went with it, was the single greatest contributor to early illness and death, and the men who scored highest on “warm relationships” earned on average $141,000 a year more at their peak than those who scored lowest.
  • A meta-analysis of 148 studies with 308,849 participants (Holt-Lunstad and colleagues, 2010) found a 50% greater likelihood of survival for people with stronger social relationships. The authors concluded that the influence of social relationships on mortality risk is comparable with well-established risk factors.

Analysis: notice what these have in common. None of it can be bought, and all of it is available today: not smoking, keeping alcohol low, and investing in the people around you. A wearable can help you notice your sleep; it cannot replace a friend.

“The best longevity hack: don’t argue”

Our view: of all the hacks on offer, the most powerful may cost nothing: stop turning disagreements into fights. The evidence is surprisingly direct.

  • Hostility slows healing. In a hospital study, Janice Kiecolt-Glaser and colleagues (2005) gave 42 married couples small blister wounds, then had them discuss a disagreement. Wounds healed more slowly after a conflict than after a supportive conversation, and couples who were consistently hostile healed at 60% of the rate of low-hostility couples.
  • Anger and hostility are linked to heart disease. A meta-analysis of prospective studies (Chida and Steptoe, 2009) found anger and hostility associated with more coronary heart disease events in initially healthy people, and with worse outcomes in people who already had it. The authors note signs of publication bias, but the association held.
  • Relationships protect. The Holt-Lunstad meta-analysis above: stronger social ties, 50% greater likelihood of survival.

Arguing in the hostile sense, with contempt, point-scoring and the need to win, wears away the relationships that the evidence says keep us alive, and seems to wear on the body directly too.

The other view: “don’t argue” should not mean “never disagree”. The studies measure hostility, not disagreement itself; swallowing every concern can damage a relationship or a team in its own way, and good engineering depends on people saying when they think something is wrong. The habit worth building is disagreeing without hostility: about the problem, not the person, and with the relationship still standing at the end (see Behaviours).

If you want to experiment

An engineer’s instincts serve well here, with one Feynman rule on top: you are the easiest person to fool (Richard Feynman; see Thinkers Worth Knowing). Analysis:

  • Change one thing at a time, and decide in advance what you will measure.
  • Be wary of anything sold by the person who recommends it.
  • Treat a “biological age” score as an estimate with error bars, not a fact.
  • Start with the boring, proven basics before the exotic.

Our view, and the other view

Our view: everyone here holds part of the picture. Biohackers are right that we can take more charge of our health and that measuring helps; doctors are right that claims need testing; and neither side has been right every time. Start with the habits all sides agree on, and treat the rest as experiments.

The other view: some say waiting for proof costs years of life, so trying promising methods early is reasonable; others say self-experiments mislead more often than they discover, and that money spent on unproven products is the real cost. Both are worth hearing before you decide for yourself.
