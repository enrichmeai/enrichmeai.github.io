Guide / AI / AI Leaders and Builders

AI leaders and AI builders: two sets of skills, one responsibility

Using AI well in a team needs people who decide where it is worth using and answer for the result, and people who can build systems with it that work. What each needs to know, where they meet, and the responsibilities they share.

The short answer

An AI leader chooses the problems worth solving, sets the rules and the budget, builds the foundations that make AI useful, and is accountable for what it does. An AI builder turns a problem into a system that works: models, context, tools, evals, guardrails, and the engineering to run it. Our view: a team needs both, often in the same person at different hours, and both carry responsibility for what the system does to the people it touches.

DORA’s 2025 research found that AI “doesn’t fix a team; it amplifies what’s already there”. Our view: that is why both roles matter. Leadership without builders produces plans; builders without leadership produce demos that solve nobody’s problem.

Source: Google Cloud: Announcing the 2025 DORA Report.

The AI leader’s skills

SkillWhat it looks like
Choosing the problemPoints AI at a clear user problem. DORA found a user-centric focus is “a prerequisite for AI success”
Setting policySays what may and may not be done with AI, and makes sure people know. DORA’s first recommendation: “Clarify and socialize your AI policies”
Investing in foundationsFunds the platform, tests and fast feedback that let AI help instead of adding instability. DORA links a quality internal platform with getting value from AI
Connecting AI to the organisation’s knowledgeMakes internal documents, data and standards available to the tools, safely (“Connect AI to your internal context”)
Judging riskKnows which uses are low-risk and which need review, law or a no. In the EU, the AI Act sorts uses into unacceptable, high, limited and minimal risk, plus a category for general-purpose AI
Measuring honestlyAsks for outcomes and evidence, not demos: delivery speed and stability, cost, and what users experienced
Growing peopleGives teams time to learn, and keeps foundations taught, so people can judge what AI produces

Analysis: our list, with the DORA findings and recommendations from Google Cloud: Announcing the 2025 DORA Report and the EU risk categories from Wikipedia: Artificial Intelligence Act.

The AI builder’s skills

SkillWhat it looks like
Engineering foundationsCode, data, APIs, distributed systems and testing. Our view: an AI system is still software, and many of its failures are ordinary software failures; the other view is that model behaviour adds failure modes ordinary testing does not catch, which is why evals exist
Knowing how models behaveContext limits, non-determinism, hallucination, cost per token; when a model is the right tool and when a rule is
Starting simpleAnthropic: find “the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed”
Context, tools and retrievalGives the model the right facts and actions at the right time; designs tools it can use correctly
EvaluationDefines success criteria that are “specific” and “measurable”, builds eval sets from real failures, and reads results rather than trusting a demo
Security and guardrailsPrompt injection, data leaks, least privilege for tools, and where a person must approve
Running itReleases, canaries, tracing, cost and latency budgets, rollback

Analysis: our list. Sources: Anthropic: Building effective agents; Claude docs: Define success criteria and build evaluations; Anthropic: Demystifying evals for AI agents. Several rows have their own page: Building an Agentic System, Agents to Production, Agentic AI Security.

Where they meet

  • The success measure. The leader says what success means for users and the business; the builder turns it into an eval that can fail.
  • The risk call. The builder shows what the system can get wrong, with evidence; the leader decides whether that is acceptable.
  • The budget. Cost per task, latency and people’s time are decided together, not discovered after launch.
  • Saying no. Either can stop a use of AI that does not hold up. A leader who cannot hear “this does not work yet” from a builder will ship a demo.

Analysis: our view.

Responsibility is shared

Microsoft’s responsible AI principles are one published example: “fairness, reliability and safety, privacy and security, inclusiveness, transparency, and accountability”. Microsoft recommends that organisations develop their own Responsible AI Standard.

  • For the leader: owning the outcome, setting the standard, and funding the time to meet it.
  • For the builder: testing for unfair or unsafe behaviour, protecting data, telling users they are dealing with AI, and being honest about limits.

Our view: law is catching up.

The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024, with obligations phased in over 6 to 36 months; limited-risk systems carry transparency duties, such as telling users they are talking to an AI.

Sources: Microsoft: Responsible AI; Wikipedia: Artificial Intelligence Act (the official text was not reachable from where this was written).

Which to grow first: choose by requirement

If this is true for youLeans towards
You decide what a team works on, or own a budgetLeader skills first, plus enough building to tell a demo from a system
You write or run softwareBuilder skills first: evals and security before agents
Your team uses AI tools but nobody owns the rulesSomeone takes the leader role now: policy, risk, measurement
Your organisation is subject to the EU AI Act or similar rulesRisk classification and transparency skills on both sides
You work in a team of a few peopleOne person often holds both roles; schedule time for each

Analysis: our view.

Our view, and the other view

Our view: a team needs both roles, and both must answer for what the system does. Leaders who can build a little make better calls; builders who understand the business build things worth keeping.

The other view: “AI leader” may just be good leadership applied to a new tool, and splitting the roles can create a layer that talks about AI without building it. In small teams the distinction may not be worth naming at all.
