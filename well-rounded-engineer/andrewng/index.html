Guide / Thinking & character / Andrew Ng

Andrew Ng: teaching AI to millions

A Stanford professor who co-founded Google Brain and Coursera, led AI at Baidu, and has taught machine learning to more people than perhaps anyone. His career, his ideas, from “AI is the new electricity” to agentic workflows, and what engineers can take from them.

The short answer

If you have taken an online course in machine learning, there is a good chance Andrew Ng taught it. He has spent his career doing two things at once: pushing AI research forward, at Stanford, Google and Baidu, and making it understandable to everyone else, through Coursera, DeepLearning.AI and his weekly newsletter, The Batch. More than 8 million people have taken his courses.

For engineers today, his most useful idea may be a simple one: an AI model gets much better results when it is allowed to work in a loop, drafting, checking and improving, than when it answers in one go. That idea is the heart of agents (see Building an Agentic System).

His career

  • Early years: born in London in 1976, he moved to Singapore with his family in 1984. He took a triple major in computer science, statistics and economics at Carnegie Mellon (1997), a master’s at MIT (1998), and a PhD at Berkeley (2002) under Michael I. Jordan.
  • Stanford: he directed the Stanford Artificial Intelligence Laboratory. His machine learning course, CS229, became the most popular on campus, with over 1,000 students in some years. His early research included the Stanford Autonomous Helicopter project.
  • Google Brain (2011–2012): he founded and directed Google’s deep learning project with Jeff Dean, Greg Corrado and Rajat Monga.
  • Coursera (2012): co-founded with Daphne Koller, after his online machine learning class drew over 100,000 registrations in its first edition in October 2011.
  • Baidu (2014): chief scientist, building research teams in areas such as facial recognition.
  • Today: he founded DeepLearning.AI and LandingAI, leads AI Fund (launched in 2018 as a $175 million fund for AI start-ups), and joined Amazon’s board in April 2024. Time named him one of its 100 most influential people in 2013, and to its Time100 AI list in 2023.

Teaching at scale

Ng’s Machine Learning course was one of the first and most successful massive open online courses. By 2019 the two most popular courses on Coursera were his, Machine Learning and Neural Networks and Deep Learning. In 2019 he launched AI for Everyone, a non-technical course on what AI means for society and business.

He credits Sal Khan of Khan Academy as a major inspiration, and the forums of Stack Overflow influenced Coursera’s design. Analysis: his teaching style, building intuition before mathematics and showing working code, is a large part of why so many engineers found machine learning approachable.

“AI is the new electricity”

In a talk at Stanford Graduate School of Business, Ng put it like this:

“Just as electricity transformed almost everything 100 years ago, today I actually have a hard time thinking of an industry that I don’t think AI will transform in the next several years.”

He named fintech, logistics, health care, security and transportation as areas ripe for change. The comparison is useful because it frames AI as a general-purpose technology, something every kind of business will use, rather than a product only technology companies sell.

Agentic workflows: his four design patterns

In March 2024 Ng wrote in The Batch that most people used language models in “zero-shot” mode: ask once, get an answer, with no chance to revise, which he likened to asking someone to compose an essay “typing straight through with no backspacing allowed”. He gathered results from several research teams on the HumanEval coding benchmark:

He then described four agentic design patterns:

PatternWhat the model does
ReflectionExamines its own work and finds ways to improve it
Tool useUses tools such as web search or code execution to gather information, take action or process data
PlanningComes up with a multi-step plan to reach a goal, and carries it out
Multi-agent collaborationSeveral agents split up a task and discuss and debate it, to reach a better answer than one agent would

He was candid about their maturity: he could get reflection and tool use to work reliably, but called planning “a less mature technology” whose behaviour is hard to predict in advance. Reflection, he wrote, is quick to implement and has led to “surprising performance gains”. Compare Anthropic’s and OpenAI’s patterns on How Anthropic & OpenAI Build Agents.

On regulation

Ng has argued for carefully designed regulation that does not block beneficial AI. In a June 2024 interview with the Financial Times he raised concerns about proposed AI legislation in California that would have placed new requirements on developers of large models.

Our view: his instinct, to regulate harmful uses rather than the underlying technology, fits how other general-purpose technologies have been handled. The other view: many people argue that powerful models carry risks that are hard to catch only at the point of use, and that rules on the developers of the largest models are a reasonable precaution. The debate is live, and reasonable people land on both sides.

What engineers can take from him

  • Let the model work in a loop: draft, check, improve. It often beats switching to a bigger model.
  • Start with the patterns that are reliable today, reflection and tool use, before planning and multi-agent systems.
  • Learn the foundations; his courses are still a good place to begin.
  • Explaining clearly is a skill that multiplies your impact.
