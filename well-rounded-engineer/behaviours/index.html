Behaviours of a well-rounded engineer

Skills are what you can do; behaviours are what you actually do every day. In the AI era the gap between them matters more: a model can produce code, but it cannot own the outcome, check the evidence, or learn from what broke. These are the behaviours that make the difference, with what they look like, and what they do not.

The headline: engaged, not prompting and resting

AI gives every engineer a bulldozer instead of a shovel. That raises the bar, not lowers it. A well-rounded engineer does not write a prompt, accept whatever comes back, and take the rest of the day off. They decide where the bulldozer goes, prepare the ground, watch it work, check the result against evidence, and fix the road for the next person. The time AI saves is spent on what it cannot do: understanding the problem, testing assumptions, reviewing, and learning.

Learn by doing, not by opinion

  • Be slow to opinion, quick to experiment. Before arguing for a tool, pattern or fix, build the smallest thing that tests it: a spike, a benchmark, a query, a prototype.
  • Let the result decide. "I ran it on 10,000 real rows and p95 went from 900 ms to 120 ms" ends a debate that opinions cannot.
  • Say what you have and have not tried. "I have read about it" and "I have run it in production" are different levels of confidence; name which one you have.
  • Change your mind when the evidence does, and say so plainly.
  • Hands on the keyboard: read the code, run the command, open the log. Second-hand summaries, including an AI's, are a starting point, not a conclusion.
  • Write down what you learned so the next person starts from your result, not from zero.

Evidence and honesty

  • Claims match evidence. "Tests pass" means you saw them pass, with the output. A check that could not run is reported as not run.
  • Say what you checked in the sentence: "no schedule in the file (searched the whole file)", not "there is no schedule".
  • "Cannot see" is not "nothing there". When a source is down, report unknown, never a confident zero.
  • Verify state, not proxies: config saying something is enabled is not proof that it is.
  • Surface bad news early: a risk raised on day one is a plan; on launch day it is an incident.

Ownership

  • You build it, you run it: care what happens after merge: deploys, alerts, users, cost.
  • Finish things: small, complete changes over large half-done ones. Done means tested, reviewed, deployed and observed.
  • Leave the system better: fix the stale doc, the flaky test, the missing alert you tripped over.
  • Turn every mistake into a guard: a test, a hook, a rule, so it cannot happen twice.
  • Know when to stop: after a few failed attempts at the same problem, step back, write down what you know, and ask, rather than thrashing.

Quality habits

  • A failing test before the fix; a passing one after.
  • Read the official docs for the version you use before relying on an API.
  • Never weaken a check to get to green.
  • Review your own diff before asking anyone else to.
  • Prefer the simplest design that meets today's need, and write down the trade-off.
  • Treat security, privacy and cost as part of "working", not extras.

Communication

  • Write clearly: PR descriptions that say what changed, why, and how it was verified; design docs others can build from.
  • Lead with the answer, then the evidence, then the detail.
  • Plain words and real numbers beat adjectives: "40% fewer bytes scanned", not "much faster".
  • Ask early and specifically: what you tried, what you saw, what you need.
  • Claim before you build: say what you are about to change so others do not duplicate it.

Collaboration

  • Review generously and honestly: catch real problems, explain why, suggest a fix; skip personal taste.
  • Assume good intent; discuss the system, not the person, especially after incidents.
  • Teach what you know: pair, document, record the trap you hit.
  • Respect decisions once made; reopen them with new evidence, not repeated opinion.
  • Make others faster: the best contribution is often a tool, template or fix that removes work for the whole team.

Breadth and curiosity

  • Learn the layer below and the layer above your own work: the database under your service, the user above your API.
  • Follow a request or a row end to end at least once: client, network, service, database, pipeline, warehouse.
  • Spend time outside your area of depth: data people learn deploys and on-call; platform people learn SQL and modelling.
  • Read incidents and postmortems from your own systems and others'.
  • Keep a list of things you do not understand yet, and work through it.

Attitude, knowledge, adaptability, persistence

Never giving up is not the same as never stopping. After a few failed attempts at the same thing, step back: write down what you tried and what you saw, change the approach, or bring someone in. The goal is never abandoned; the method is changed.

"I'll learn it", not just "I don't know"

When you meet something new, answer with what you will do, honestly.

Instead ofSay
"I don't know Kafka.""I haven't used Kafka in production yet. I learn quickly: I'll run it locally this week and come back with what I find."
"Not my area.""I don't know that part of the system yet. Who owns it? I'll read the code and pair with them."
Bluffing an answer"I'm not sure. I'll check the docs and confirm by end of day."
  • Be exact about your current level: honesty about what you have not done is what makes "I learn quickly" believable.
  • Follow through: a promise to learn that is kept builds more trust than pretending to know.
  • Show the evidence of learning: a lab, a spike, a written summary.

Behaviours by level

1 Foundation

  • Asks for help with specifics: what was tried and seen
  • Tests own changes; reports results honestly
  • Reviews AI output line by line before committing
  • Writes down what they learn

2 Practitioner

  • Owns features from design to production and after
  • Proves ideas with spikes and measurements before arguing
  • Turns repeated mistakes into tests and rules
  • Gives useful, kind reviews

3 Senior

  • Makes the evidence standard for the team ("what did you check?")
  • Builds the blueprints and guardrails others use, including for AI agents
  • Raises risks early and stops work that is going wrong
  • Mentors through pairing and written examples

4 Expert

  • Shapes culture: blameless learning, honest status, small safe changes
  • Changes direction when the evidence says so, publicly
  • Multiplies the organisation through platforms, standards and teaching
  • Holds the line on quality when deadlines push against it

Weekly self-check

  • Did every claim I made this week have evidence behind it?
  • Did I try something myself before forming an opinion on it?
  • Did I review and understand all the AI-written code I shipped?
  • Did I leave one thing better than I found it?
  • Did I turn a mistake into a guard?
  • Did I learn something outside my usual area?
  • Did I help someone else get faster?

Questions to ask in reviews and interviews

  • "How do you know it works?" (looking for evidence, not confidence)
  • "What did you try before deciding?" (looking for experiments)
  • "What would make you change your mind?"
  • "Walk me through what the AI produced and why you kept or changed it."
  • "Tell me about something you broke and what you changed afterwards."
  • "What happens if this dependency is down?"
