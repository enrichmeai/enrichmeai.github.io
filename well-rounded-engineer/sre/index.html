Site reliability engineering

Running production as an engineering problem: reliability targets agreed with the business, error budgets that balance speed and stability, alerting on what users feel, incident response, blameless learning, and removing toil. Signals and tools are on Observability.

Principles

  • 100% is the wrong target. Users cannot tell 99.99% from 100%, and chasing it stops all change. Pick the reliability users actually need.
  • Service level objectives make that target explicit and measurable.
  • Error budgets turn it into a decision rule: budget left, ship faster; budget spent, slow down and fix reliability.
  • Toil is reduced by engineering: automate repetitive manual work rather than staffing it.
  • Blameless postmortems: incidents are system failures to learn from, not people to blame.
  • Simplicity: every component you do not have cannot fail.
  • No person or laptop is the mechanism: if something only works while a human watches it, it is not built yet.

SLI, SLO, SLA, error budget

TermIsExample
SLIA measured ratio of good events to valid eventsSearch requests answered successfully in under 500 ms ÷ all search requests
SLOThe target for an SLI over a window99.5% over 28 days
Error budget1 − SLO: the failure you may spend0.5% of requests ≈ 3.4 hours of full outage in 28 days

Measure SLIs as close to the user as possible (load balancer or client), not from inside the server, which cannot see its own outages.

Error budget policy

  • Budget healthy: ship at normal speed; take calculated risks.
  • Budget at risk (e.g. 50% spent early): extra review on risky changes; reliability work moves up the backlog.
  • Budget exhausted: freeze non-urgent feature releases for the service until reliability fixes land and the budget recovers.
  • One incident ate over 20% of the budget: a postmortem with tracked actions is mandatory.
  • Agree the policy with product owners before it is needed.

Alerting on SLOs: burn rates

Burn rate = how fast you are spending the budget, relative to spending exactly all of it over the window. Burn rate 1 uses the budget in exactly 28–30 days; burn rate 14.4 uses 2% of a 30-day budget in one hour.

Both windows must exceed the threshold: the long one shows it matters, the short one shows it is still happening, so the alert stops soon after recovery. These are the starting values from Google's SRE Workbook; tune to your traffic.

On-call

  • Sustainable load: few enough pages that each one gets real attention; track pages per shift.
  • Every page has a runbook: what it means, how to check, how to mitigate, who to escalate to.
  • Primary and secondary on rotation, with handover notes.
  • Access ready in advance: break-glass roles, dashboards, tooling; never "I need permissions" at 3 a.m.
  • Reduce pages continuously: every page should lead to a fix, a tuned alert, or an automated response.

Incident response

RoleDoes
Incident commanderOwns the incident, coordinates, decides; does not debug

Communications leadUpdates stakeholders and users on a schedule
ScribeKeeps the timeline: what was seen, decided, done, when
  1. Declare early; set severity; open one channel.
  2. Mitigate first: roll back, fail over, disable the feature, shed load.
  3. Communicate status regularly, including "no change".
  4. Resolve, confirm with the SLIs, then hand over to the postmortem.

Blameless postmortem template

# Incident: <title>            Sev: 2     Date: 2026-11-12
## Summary          what happened, impact, duration (2–3 sentences)
## Impact           users affected, SLO budget spent, data affected
## Timeline (UTC)   detection, escalation, mitigation, resolution
## Root cause(s)    the conditions that allowed it, not a person
## Detection        how we found out; how we should have
## What went well
## What went badly
## Where we got lucky
## Action items     owner · due date · ticket — prevent, detect, mitigate
## Lessons          what we now know about the system

Ask "what made this possible?" five times, not "who did it?". Turn each lesson into a guard: a test, an alert, a policy, an automation.

Toil

  • Toil is manual, repetitive, automatable, reactive work that grows with the service and leaves no lasting value: restarting jobs by hand, re-running loads, copying credentials.
  • Cap it (Google aims for under 50% of an SRE's time) and spend the rest on engineering that removes it.
  • Measure it: log toil tasks and their time; automate the biggest first.
  • Automation must be durable: a scheduler and a job in the platform, not a script on someone's laptop or a session polling in a loop.

Production readiness review

  • SLOs defined, measured and on a dashboard.
  • Alerts on SLO burn and on missing runs, each with a runbook; proven to fire.
  • Logs structured, traces flowing, dashboards from the template.
  • Capacity tested at expected peak plus headroom.
  • Deploys automated, with canary or rollback tested.
  • Backups exist and a restore has been tested; RPO and RTO written down.
  • Dependencies listed with their failure behaviour.
  • Security review done; secrets in a manager; least-privilege IAM.
  • On-call rotation and escalation path in place.

Reliability practices

  • Progressive delivery: canaries, feature flags and automatic rollback.
  • Capacity planning: forecast from growth, load-test to find limits, keep headroom.
  • Graceful degradation: serve partial or cached results and say so.
  • Disaster recovery: multi-zone by default; multi-region when the SLO needs it; rehearsed restores.
  • Game days and chaos tests: break things on purpose in a controlled way to test detection and response.
  • Dependency SLOs: your SLO cannot exceed what your critical dependencies provide unless you add redundancy.

SLOs on Google Cloud

  • Cloud Monitoring service monitoring defines services and SLOs (request-based or window-based) and charts budget.
  • Alert policies can use the SLO burn rate directly.
  • Declare SLOs and alerts in Terraform (google_monitoring_slo, google_monitoring_alert_policy) so they are reviewed and versioned.
  • For pipelines, compute freshness and completeness SLIs from run records in BigQuery and export them as custom or log-based metrics.

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Explain SLI, SLO, error budget and SLA
  • Follow a runbook and escalate during an incident
  • Contribute to a blameless postmortem

2 Practitioner

  • Define SLIs and SLOs for a service or pipeline
  • Set up burn-rate alerts and runbooks
  • Take on-call shifts and lead mitigation

3 Senior

  • Act as incident commander; run postmortems and track actions
  • Write and enforce an error budget policy with product owners
  • Run production readiness reviews and capacity plans

4 Expert

  • Set reliability strategy and SRE practice for the organisation
  • Design multi-region resilience and DR programmes
  • Build a culture of blameless learning and toil reduction

Each level includes the ones before it. See Start here for what the levels mean.
