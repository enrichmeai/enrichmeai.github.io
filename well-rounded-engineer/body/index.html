Body, mind and nature

We get one life, and the body we live it in. Work is a large part of that life, whatever the job: an engineer, a nurse, a teacher, a driver, a shopkeeper. This tab is about the person doing the work: how your nervous system and body respond to the day, how to look after them, and how we belong to the natural world rather than stand outside it. Look after these, and the journey gets more beautiful, and the work gets better too.

One life: make the journey beautiful

  • Your job is part of your life, not all of it. The habits on this page help whatever work you do, and they help most in the parts of life that are not work at all.
  • Notice the good things, however small: a conversation, a problem solved, a walk, a meal shared. Noticing is a habit, and it can be practised.
  • Spend time on what you will be glad of later: the people you love, your health, learning, making something, helping someone.
  • Be kind to yourself as well as others. You will have bad days. A bad day is a day, not a verdict on you.

Your nervous system, in plain words

  • The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system is the network of nerves that carries signals between them and the rest of the body.
  • The autonomic nervous system runs what you do not think about: heartbeat, breathing, digestion. It has two main branches that balance each other.
  • Sympathetic: "fight or flight". Heart faster, breathing quicker, muscles ready, digestion paused. Useful in a real emergency.
  • Parasympathetic: "rest and digest". Heart slower, breathing deeper, digestion and repair. This is where recovery happens.
  • Both are normal. The aim is not to switch one off, but to move between them, and not to stay stuck in alarm.

Stress: an alarm that should switch off

  • Your body cannot tell a deadline from a danger. A page at 2am, a tense meeting, an angry message: each can start the same stress response, with adrenaline and cortisol.
  • Short bursts are fine, and can sharpen you. The harm comes when the alarm never switches off: sleep, mood, focus and health all suffer.
  • Notice your signals: a tight jaw or shoulders, shallow breathing, short temper, waking at night thinking about work.
  • Slow your breathing, with the out-breath longer than the in-breath. Breathing is one of the few parts of the autonomic system you can change on purpose, and many people find a few slow breaths calming.
  • Move, sleep, and talk to someone. If stress or low mood lasts for weeks, speak to a doctor or a professional. Asking for help is a strength.

Sleep is part of the work

  • Sleep is when the brain consolidates what you learned during the day. Cutting sleep to learn more works against you.
  • Most adults need at least seven hours, as the major health bodies advise. Needs vary; how you feel during the day is a good guide.
  • Keep regular times for sleeping and waking, even at weekends where you can. Your body clock likes routine.
  • Get daylight in the morning and dim the lights and screens in the evening. Light is the main signal that sets your body clock.
  • Watch late caffeine. Its effects can last for hours after the last cup.
  • On-call nights are real costs. Plan recovery after them, as you would after any hard physical shift.

Your brain keeps changing

  • The brain changes with what you do. Learning and practice strengthen connections between neurons throughout life. This is called neuroplasticity, and it is why "I will learn it" is true at any age.
  • Spread practice out. Studying in several shorter sessions over days is remembered better than one long cram. This is the spacing effect.
  • Test yourself rather than rereading. Recalling something from memory strengthens it more than looking at it again.
  • Learn by doing. Building the thing, breaking it and fixing it engages far more of you than watching someone else do it.

Attention is a limited resource

  • Focus tires, like a muscle. Plan your hardest thinking for the time of day you are sharpest.
  • Every interruption has a cost: getting back into a hard problem takes time. Turn off notifications you do not need, and batch messages.
  • Work in blocks, then take a real break: stand up, look away from the screen, step outside.
  • One thing at a time. Switching between tasks feels productive and usually is not.

Look after the body you work in

  • Rest your eyes. Eye-care bodies suggest the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 metres) away for 20 seconds. Remember to blink.
  • Change position often. No single posture is perfect for hours; the best posture is the next one. Set the screen at eye level and keep your wrists relaxed.
  • Move every day (see Being a Good Human): it helps your heart, your mood, your sleep and your thinking.
  • Drink water, eat real food, and do not let the day run on snacks and caffeine.
  • Protect your hearing: keep headphones at a moderate volume.
  • Listen to pain. Wrist, neck or back pain that keeps coming back is a signal to change something, and to see a professional.

Mother nature

We are part of nature, not visitors to it. The air, water, food and climate that every job depends on come from it, and we are the only ones who can look after it.

  • Go outside. Time in green and blue spaces (parks, woods, rivers, the sea) is linked in research with better wellbeing. A walk outside is one of the cheapest good things you can do for yourself.
  • Nature is the original systems engineer. Ecosystems run on feedback loops, recycle everything, spread risk through diversity and redundancy, and grow within limits. Those are good lessons for any system you build.
  • Learn from its designs. Engineers call this biomimicry: Velcro came from burdock burrs catching on a dog's fur, and the nose of Japan's 500 Series Shinkansen was shaped after a kingfisher's beak to reduce noise in tunnels.
  • Notice the seasons. Energy, light and mood change through the year. Planning with them, rather than against them, is kinder to people.

Care for the planet in your work

  • Computing uses real energy and water. Every server, model and stored copy has a footprint, even when nobody looks at it.
  • Turn off what you do not use: idle machines, forgotten environments, old snapshots, data nobody reads. It saves money and energy together.
  • Right-size: pick the smallest machine and the simplest design that does the job, and scale to zero where you can.
  • Choose cleaner regions where you have the choice. Cloud providers publish how much of each region's electricity comes from carbon-free sources.
  • Use AI where it helps, not by reflex. A large model is a heavy tool; a query, a script or a small model is often enough.

None of this is about guilt. It is about being a good guest on a planet we share, in the same spirit as being a good colleague.

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Explain fight-or-flight and rest-and-digest in plain words
  • Keep regular sleep, daily movement and real breaks
  • Notice your own stress signals

2 Practitioner

  • Plan hard thinking for your best hours; protect focus
  • Recover after on-call and crunch periods
  • Switch off idle resources you own

3 Senior

  • Set a sustainable pace and humane on-call for a team
  • Spot burnout signals in others and act early
  • Design systems that are lean in energy and cost

4 Expert

  • Shape a culture where health and rest are normal
  • Make sustainability part of architecture decisions
  • Model a full life, not only a full calendar

Each level includes the ones before it. See Start here for what the levels mean.
