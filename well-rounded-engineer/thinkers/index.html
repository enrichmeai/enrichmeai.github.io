Thinkers worth knowing

Five people whose ideas keep turning up in engineering teams, often second-hand and sometimes garbled: Peter Drucker on effectiveness, Tim Ferriss on doing less, Naval Ravikant on leverage, Richard Feynman on not fooling yourself, and David Deutsch on good explanations. What each actually said, in their own words where we could find them, and where to be careful.

The short answer

You have probably heard all five of these names in a meeting, a podcast or a slide deck, usually attached to a single quotable line. That is a pity, because each of them offers something more useful than a slogan: a way of looking at work, money, evidence or knowledge that you can actually use on a Tuesday afternoon.

This page gives each of them a fair hearing. Where we could read their own words, we quote them; where a famous line turns out to be someone else’s, we say so; and each card ends with the honest caveat, because every one of these thinkers has thoughtful critics too.

Peter Drucker: do the right things

Peter Drucker spent his career thinking about how organisations and the people in them actually get things done. In 1959 he coined the term knowledge worker, someone whose main contribution is what they know and think rather than what they make with their hands, and later in life he called knowledge-worker productivity the next frontier of management. If you write software for a living, he was writing about you, decades early.

His most useful idea for engineers is the difference between being efficient and being effective. In a 1963 Harvard Business Review article, “Managing for Business Effectiveness”, he wrote: “There is surely nothing quite so useless as doing with great efficiency what should not be done at all.” Wikiquote notes that the idea is older than Drucker, with a similar line about education in 1887, but he made it famous. In The Effective Executive he added a line any team lead will recognise: “Morale in an organization does not mean that ‘people get along together’; the test is performance not conformance.”

Be careful with: “Culture eats strategy for breakfast.” It is everywhere with Drucker’s name on it, but Wikiquote lists it under misattributed. When you quote Drucker, quote something he wrote.

On this site: “Busy is not progress” on Ways of Working puts the same idea into a daily routine.

Tim Ferriss: DEAL, a recipe for doing less

Tim Ferriss wrote The 4-Hour Workweek (2007) after running his sports nutrition company on 14-hour days, taking a three-week break abroad, and coming back with a different way of working: email once a day, small tasks outsourced. The book spent four years on the New York Times best-seller list. In his own words, “The 4-Hour Workweek is broken into Definition, Elimination, Automation, and Liberation”, which spells DEAL.

StepWhat Ferriss meansWhat it looks like for an engineer (analysis)
DefinitionDecide what you actually want to do, be and have, and what that costs. Then find which few efforts produce most of the results, using the 80/20 or Pareto principle: “20 percent of your inputs, will produce 80 percent of your desired results.”Find the few features, services or tasks that matter most to users, and the ones nobody would miss
EliminationCut what is left, and use Parkinson’s law: limit the time you give a task “so that you limit yourself to the truly important”. Batch similar tasks such as email into a few fixed times, and do the one most important task before lunch.Fewer meetings, notifications batched, one protected block each morning
AutomationOutsource and delegate; in his 2007 talk he suggested outsourcing “anything that can be done for less than $25”. He also describes testing a product idea cheaply before building it, which he later compared to what we now call a minimum viable product.Scripts, CI pipelines and bots for repeated work; prototypes before platforms
LiberationFirst, mobility: getting out of the office, even part-time, so time saved is really yours. Second, deciding what to do with the time you have created.Remote and flexible work, and the harder question of what to do with an hour you have won back

Be careful with: the title. The New York Times noted that Ferriss himself spends far more than four hours a week on blogging and promotion, Wired faulted the book’s formulaic writing while praising its ideas on remote work, and some critics have been harsher. Treat DEAL as a set of questions to ask about your week, not a promise about its length.

Naval Ravikant: leverage and specific knowledge

Naval Ravikant co-founded AngelList in 2010 and invested early in companies including Uber and Twitter. Most people know his thinking through a long thread of short posts called “How to Get Rich (Without Getting Lucky)”, which Eric Jorgenson collected, with Naval’s permission, into The Almanack of Naval Ravikant (2020), alongside his essays and interviews. The ideas that matter most for engineers:

  • Specific knowledge. “Specific knowledge is knowledge you cannot be trained for. If society can train you, it can train someone else and replace you.” It “is found by pursuing your genuine curiosity and passion rather than whatever is hot right now”, and “will feel like play to you but will look like work to others.”
  • Leverage. “Fortunes require leverage. Business leverage comes from capital, people, and products with no marginal cost of replication (code and media).” Capital and labour need someone’s permission; “code and media are permissionless leverage.” Every engineer already holds one of the most powerful levers there is.
  • Long-term games. “Play iterated games. All the returns in life, whether in wealth, relationships, or knowledge, come from compound interest.”
  • Build and sell. “Learn to sell. Learn to build. If you can do both, you will be unstoppable.”

Be careful with: survivorship. Advice from someone who succeeded is shaped by the paths that worked for them; plenty of people followed similar principles without the same results (analysis). The ideas about learning, leverage and long-term relationships hold up well on their own; the promise in the title is the part to hold loosely.

Richard Feynman: you must not fool yourself

Richard Feynman shared the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics for work on quantum electrodynamics, worked on the atomic bomb during the Second World War, and became known to the wider public in the 1980s as a member of the Rogers Commission that investigated the Challenger disaster. But the reason engineers keep quoting him is a speech about honesty.

In his 1974 commencement address at Caltech, later published as “Cargo Cult Science”, he warned graduates not to play down the weaknesses of their own work in pursuit of a conclusion they wanted. His line from it belongs on every engineering team’s wall: “The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool.” A load test you designed to pass, a benchmark that measures the cache rather than the code, a demo that only works on your laptop: each is a small way of fooling yourself.

The other line, found on his blackboard when he died in February 1988, is the best one-sentence description of learning by building: “What I cannot create, I do not understand.” It is why this guide pairs its explanations with code you can run.

On this site: “From counting to algebra, the Feynman way” on Mathematics, from The Feynman Lectures on Physics.

David Deutsch: good explanations, and why problems are soluble

David Deutsch is a physicist at the University of Oxford, often described as the “father of quantum computing”: he described a quantum Turing machine and specified an algorithm designed to run on a quantum computer (more on Quantum Computing). Since 2012 he has worked on constructor theory, which states the laws of physics in terms of which transformations are possible and which are impossible.

His book The Beginning of Infinity (2011) argues that all progress, scientific, philosophical or cultural, comes from the search for good explanations. His test for a good explanation is one every engineer can use: it should be hard to vary. An explanation is hard to vary when every detail does real work, so you cannot change a part without breaking the whole. His example is the ancient story that the seasons happen because the goddess Demeter is sad; it would work just as well if she were happy, which is exactly why it explains nothing. The modern explanation, the Earth’s tilted axis as it orbits the Sun, has no detail you could swap.

The same test works on a postmortem. “The service failed because of high load” could explain almost any outage, so it explains none in particular. “The connection pool held 20 connections, the new query took 2 seconds, so at 10 requests a second the pool ran dry” is hard to vary, and that is why you can act on it (analysis). And Deutsch’s optimism, that problems are inevitable but solutions exist if the right knowledge is sought, is a healthy stance for anyone staring at a hard bug.

How they fit together

Read side by side, the five answer different questions, which is why they are worth knowing together rather than as rival gurus (analysis):

If you are asking…Start with
Am I working on the right thing?Drucker: effectiveness before efficiency
How do I get my time back?Ferriss: Definition, Elimination, Automation, Liberation
What should I build my career on?Naval: specific knowledge, leverage, long-term games
Can I trust this result?Feynman: you must not fool yourself
Do I actually understand why it happened?Deutsch: an explanation that is hard to vary

Our view, and the other view

Our view: read the originals, not the quote cards. Each of these people said something more careful and more useful than the line that travels with their name, and the habit of checking who really said what is itself the Feynman principle at work.

The other view: two of the five are writers about success whose advice is shaped by their own unusual paths, and even the scientists are quoted far outside their fields. Many good engineers never read any of them; the ideas matter only if they change what you do next week.
