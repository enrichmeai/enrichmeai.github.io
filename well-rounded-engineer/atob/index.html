Guide / Architecture & design / Solution Architecture: A to B

Solution architecture: getting from A to B

A picture of the final system is the easy part. The engineering is in the route: which steps, in what order, what runs in between, what is built only to be thrown away, and how each step can be undone. This page separates solution architecture from business, technical and infrastructure architecture, names three kinds of solution (strategic, tactical and temporary), and shows an incremental model from today’s system (A) to the target (B). The patterns themselves are on Architecture.

Solution architecture is not business architecture

Business architectureSolution architectureEnterprise architecture
Definition (sourced)Holistic views of the business: capabilities, end-to-end value delivery, information and organisational structure, and how they relate to strategies, products, policies and stakeholders (wording quoted on Wikipedia)A description of a discrete, focused business operation or activity and how information systems and technology support it (The Open Group’s definition, among several)An enterprise architecture framework is a methodology for guiding the transformation of a business from a baseline state to a target state, sometimes through several transition states
The question it answers (analysis)What does the business do, and what must it be able to do?How will these systems change, in what order, to support that?How does the whole estate move, and what standards hold it together?
Typical output (analysis)Capability maps, value streamsBaseline, transition and target states; interfaces; data ownership; decisions and their trade-offsRoadmaps, principles, the portfolio

Our view: a solution architecture that only restates business capabilities, or only draws the target, is not finished. It must say how the systems change, step by step, and what runs at each step. The other view: in a small organisation one person does all three, and the lines blur; and a solution designed without the business architecture can be a fine route to the wrong place.

Solution, technical and infrastructure architecture

TOGAF, an enterprise architecture framework, models architecture in four domains: business, data, application and technology. TOGAF’s skills framework does not recognise a solution architect role, yet such roles are widely advertised, and the technical and infrastructure split below is common usage rather than TOGAF, so the same words can mean different jobs. The last column uses an invented example: keeping a shopping cart the same on every device.

LayerDefined as (TOGAF, as summarised on Wikipedia)Decides, in practice (analysis)Shopping-cart example (illustrative)
BusinessThe business strategy, governance, organisation and key business processesWhat the business must be able to doCustomers expect the same cart on every device
SolutionNot one of TOGAF’s four domains. The Open Group defines it as “a description of a discrete and focused business operation or activity and how IS/IT supports that operation”Which systems change, their interfaces, who owns which data, and the route from A to BAdd a cart events topic and a push gateway, in transition steps
ApplicationA blueprint for the individual systems to be deployed, their interactions, and their relationship to core business processesThe services and how they talkThe cart service, and a backend-for-frontend that holds browser connections
DataThe structure of the organisation’s logical and physical data assets, and the data management resources that go with themSources of truth, models, retentionThe cart service owns the cart; events are keyed by cart id
Technical (TOGAF: technology)The hardware, software and network infrastructure needed to support the deployment of core, mission-critical applicationsCommon usage, not TOGAF: many organisations split technology in two. Technical: the software platform, meaning languages, frameworks, middleware and integration patternsKafka, server-sent events, the web framework
InfrastructureNo separate TOGAF domain: infrastructure appears inside the technology domain’s definitionCommon usage, not TOGAF: Infrastructure: compute, networks, storage, hosting, regions and recoveryThe managed Kafka cluster, load-balancer timeouts long enough for open connections, two regions

Our view: solution architecture cuts across the other layers. It starts from the business need, chooses the application and data changes, asks the technical and infrastructure architects for platforms that fit, and puts it all in order from A to B; Gartner’s definition, which combines guidance from business, information and technical viewpoints, is similar. The other view: in organisations with strong platform teams, the solution architect mostly chooses from a catalogue that technical and infrastructure architects maintain; and in a small team one engineer holds every layer at once.

Strategic, tactical and temporary: say which one you are building

KindWhat it is forWhat you accept (analysis)When it ends (analysis)
StrategicPart of the target state (B), built to lastHigher cost and time up frontNot planned; it is the destination
TacticalMeets a real need now, inside today’s constraints (a deadline, a regulation, a contract)Known shortcuts: prudent, deliberate technical debt in Fowler’s terms, taken on knowingly, when the payoff for an earlier release outweighs the cost of paying it backWhen the strategic solution replaces it; the date and the trigger are written down
Temporary (transitional)Exists only to help move from A to B: Cartwright, Horn and Lewis call it transitional architecture, “software elements installed to ease the displacement of a legacy system that we intend to remove when the displacement is complete”Work that will be thrown away, by designWhen the transition state it serves is passed

Analysis: the three-way split, and mapping tactical to prudent, deliberate debt and temporary to transitional architecture, are our framing, not a standard. The sources use the debt quadrant and transitional architecture, not these three labels.

  • Technical debt is Ward Cunningham’s metaphor (1992); Fowler’s gloss is that the extra effort every later change costs because of a shortcut is the interest on the debt.
  • Sacrificial architecture (Fowler) is the longer-horizon version of the same idea: deliberately accepting now that in a few years you will (hopefully) need to throw away what you are building.

Our view: label every component strategic, tactical or temporary, and give each tactical and temporary one an owner, an exit condition and a date. The other view: labels drift, and many “temporary” solutions become permanent; some teams refuse tactical work for exactly that reason and accept slower delivery instead.

The incremental model: A, transition states, B

Cartwright, Horn and Lewis, in Patterns of Legacy Displacement, put it plainly: gradual replacement delivers benefits early and avoids the risks of a big bang, and while it runs the old and new systems operate side by side. An illustrative example, moving order handling off a legacy system:

StateWhat runsBuilt only for this stateValue deliveredWay back
A: baselineThe legacy system does everything–––
Transition 1A router in front of the legacy system; all traffic still goes to legacyThe router, if B will not need itA seam: you can now move trafficRemove the router
Transition 2New order-status service; legacy still owns the data; state changes copied to the new serviceThe copy of events from legacyFaster order tracking for customersRoute status back to legacy
Transition 3New system owns new orders; legacy keeps old orders until they closeA sync that keeps legacy reports workingNew orders on the new platformFeature toggle sends new orders to legacy
B: targetNew system only–Legacy licence and support cost removedNone needed: decommission is the last deliverable

Each transition state is a real production system: it must be operated, monitored and secured, and it must say which system is the source of truth for each piece of data. When you draw your own, add a “source of truth” column for each state. The example is illustrative, not a recommendation for any real system.

Short-term solutions for the transition period

During the transition you need to…TechniqueRemoved when (analysis)
Move features one at a time from old to newStrangler fig: new pieces grow around the old system until it can be retiredThe last feature has moved
Change an interface many callers use, without breaking themParallel change (expand and contract): expand, migrate, contractThe contract phase
Replace a component inside one codebase while still releasing regularlyBranch by abstraction: put an abstraction in front, build the new implementation behind it, switchThe old implementation is deleted
Ship unfinished work safelyRelease toggles: transitionary by nature; Hodgson says they should generally not stay much longer than a week or two, though product-centric toggles may stay longerThe feature is fully released
Change things without the old system noticingLegacy mimic: the new system interacts with the legacy one so that it is not aware of any changeThe legacy system is gone
Feed the new system from the old one’s changesEvent interception: intercept updates to system state and route some of them to the new componentThe new system owns that data
Prove the new path under real load before users see itDark launching: call the new back end from existing traffic without users being able to tellThe new path is live
Expose the change to a few users firstCanary release: roll out to a small subset before everyoneRolled out to all

Analysis: most moves combine several of these. A strangler fig moves the traffic, an anti-corruption layer protects the new model, and a parallel run proves the result before the old path is switched off.

Parallel run

The old and new systems run side by side for an agreed period, take the same input, do the same processing, and their outputs are compared. When the new system has proved reliable, the old one is removed. Cartwright, Horn and Lewis list it among the ways to cut over from a legacy system.

  • The cost: running two systems at once is expensive, takes time, and can mean doing work twice.
  • Decide before you start (analysis): which outputs must match exactly, what tolerance applies to the rest, how long the run lasts, and which system’s answer counts while they disagree.

Anti-corruption layer

A mediation layer that translates one system’s domain model into another’s, so the new service is not shaped by the old one’s meanings. AWS suggests it when the migrated service’s model differs from the original, when two systems with different semantics must exchange data, or when changing one to fit the other is not practical.

  • What it costs: another component to monitor, release and run; added latency; a possible scaling bottleneck; and a single point of failure, because if it fails the service behind it is unreachable. AWS says to record it as technical debt and decommission it once every dependent caller has migrated.
  • What it buys: callers owned by other teams need not change at the same time as you.

Dual write, and the two ways out

A dual write is one operation that writes to two systems, such as saving to the database and then publishing an event, or writing to both the old and the new database during a migration. If one write succeeds and the other fails, the two disagree, and nothing notices unless you check. AWS describes two ways out, both under the transactional outbox pattern: an outbox table, or change data capture.

Way outHow it worksFits (analysis)
Transactional outboxWrite the change and an outbox row in one database transaction, then publish from the outbox (see Architecture); events can arrive twice or out of order, so make the consumer idempotentYour own service, where you can change the code
Change data captureRead each committed row-level change from the database and publish it as an event. Debezium does this, and only committed changes are visible, so rolled-back work never leaks outA legacy database you cannot change, or many tables at once

Our view: do not dual write; use one of the two above. The other view, in our experience: some teams accept a dual write for a short migration window, with a reconciliation job that compares the two stores every night, because it needs no new infrastructure. If you do, treat every mismatch as an incident, and write down the date the dual write ends.

Splitting a shared database

  • The target AWS describes: database per service, where each service stores and reads only its own data, so services stay loosely coupled and each can choose its store. What it costs: cross-service transactions and queries are harder, and you run several databases.
  • The shared-database pattern: several services use one database. AWS says to avoid hot tables (single tables that several services write to) and to keep every schema change backward-compatible: drop a column or table only when no current or previous version of any service references it. AWS also notes it does not reduce dependencies between teams and adds runtime coupling.
  • Queries across services: AWS says that with database per service you must add another pattern for these: API composition (which CQRS can speed up) or event sourcing.

Analysis: a common order is: give each table one owning module while the database is still shared; stop other modules reading those tables directly, going through the owner instead; copy the data to the new store with change data capture; switch reads, then writes; and finally drop the old tables. Every step before the drop can be reversed; plan the drop last, after the old tables have gone unreferenced for an agreed period.

Engineering from A to B, not just a picture of B

Our view: rules of thumb, not a standard.

  • Every step deploys and runs on its own, and has a way back.
  • Every step delivers something a user or the business can see, or removes a cost; otherwise ask why it is a step.
  • Data has one owner at each state. Write down which system is the source of truth, and when that changes.
  • Budget for the throwaway work. Transitional pieces are real engineering, then real removal.
  • Decommissioning is a deliverable, with a date and an owner, not a hope.
  • Record each choice in an architecture decision record, including which items are tactical or temporary (see Architecture).

Our view, and the other view

Our view: a proposal that shows only the final state is a wish, not a plan. The useful document shows A, each transition state, B, what is temporary at each point and how it goes away.

The other view: a clear target picture is what aligns people and wins funding, and the detailed route is often better worked out by the delivery teams as they learn; fixing every step up front can lock in a route that turns out wrong. Both can hold: fix the target and the first one or two transitions, and plan the rest as you go.

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Tell business, solution and enterprise architecture apart
  • Say whether a piece of work is strategic, tactical or temporary
  • Draw today's system (A) and the target (B)

2 Practitioner

  • Plan transition states between A and B, each one deployable
  • Use strangler fig, parallel change or branch by abstraction for a real change
  • Write down the source of truth for each piece of data at each state

3 Senior

  • Design transitional architecture and plan its removal
  • Take on tactical debt deliberately, with an owner and an exit date
  • Lead a legacy displacement without a big bang

4 Expert

  • Set how an organisation plans and funds transitions, not only targets
  • Govern a portfolio of strategic, tactical and temporary work
  • Teach teams to engineer the route, not only the destination

Each level includes the ones before it. See Start here for what the levels mean.
