Banking systems and low latency

Banking software is judged first on correctness and availability, and only then on speed; trading systems are judged on speed in microseconds. This page covers how a bank's systems fit together, what changed when payments became instant, and how low latency is actually achieved and measured. The money-handling rules themselves (double-entry, idempotency, reconciliation) are on Payment Gateways; percentiles and profiling are on Performance.

What makes banking software different

Analysis, built on the sources at the end. A shop that loses an order apologises. A bank that loses or invents money breaks the law. That changes the order of priorities:

  1. Correct: money is never created or lost. Every movement is a balanced pair of entries (see Payment Gateways), and every change can be traced to who made it and why.
  2. Available: instant payment schemes run 24 hours a day, every day, so there is no longer a quiet night to take systems down.
  3. Fast enough: fast enough for the scheme’s deadline and for the customer, which is usually far from the microseconds a trading system needs.

The other view: some argue that speed is itself a form of correctness for payments, because a payment that misses its scheme deadline fails. Both views agree on the order inside the deadline: correct first.

How a bank’s systems fit together

  • Channels: the mobile app, web banking, branches, call centres, and partners through APIs.
  • Integration: gateways and messaging that connect channels to the systems of record, with limits, fraud checks and audit on the way.
  • Core banking: the system that centralises the bank’s record keeping for deposits, loans and accounts, so any branch or channel sees one record.
  • Payment schemes: connections to card networks, instant payment schemes and slower batch schemes, each with its own message formats and deadlines.
  • Ledger and reporting: the general ledger, regulatory reports, reconciliation.
  • Mainframes and COBOL: COBOL is used mainly in business, finance and administrative systems, and is still widely used on mainframes for large-scale batch and transaction processing; most COBOL work today maintains existing applications. Some IBM mainframes (z900 to System z10) execute instructions twice and compare the results, to catch hardware faults.

What changes when payments are instant (analysis): checks that once ran in the overnight batch, such as limits, fraud and sanctions screening, now run inline within the deadline; and maintenance can no longer wait for a closed weekend.

Why the new banks got to market fast: Monzo, Revolut, Starling

Three UK-founded banks went from start-up to millions of customers in about a decade. The published record points to three reasons, and only one of them is technology.

ReasonWhat happened
The rules openedAfter the 2008 financial crisis the UK government decided to open the market to new banks. The Financial Services Act 2012 came into force on 1 April 2013, and the Prudential Regulation Authority set up a New Bank Start-up Unit with a staged route: an expression of interest, then “authorisation with restrictions”, then the restrictions lifted. Before that, setting up a bank with a full UK licence was extremely expensive and time-consuming, and the “Big Four” banks dominated. Starling, founded in January 2014, received its licence in July 2016; Monzo’s restrictions were lifted in April 2017.
They started narrowMonzo (founded as Mondo in 2015) began with an app and a prepaid card, and launched its full current account in October 2017. Revolut (founded in July 2015) began as an app for spending abroad at interbank exchange rates, as an e-money institution rather than a bank; it received an EU banking licence in December 2018 and a UK licence, with restrictions, in 2024. Each offered one useful thing first, and became a bank later.
No legacy to carryNo branches (Starling has none) and app-first products, which Wikipedia’s article on challenger banks describes as modern practices “that avoid the costs and complexities of traditional banking”. Starling built its own platform and in February 2022 set up Engine, a subsidiary that offers its systems to other firms as a service.

The cost of moving fast. In 2019 Monzo said it had inadvertently logged 480,000 customers’ card PINs, readable by its own engineers, because they were sent in the query string of an HTTPS request. In September 2022 a cyber attack on Revolut exposed the personal data of over 50,000 customers, and a flaw in its US payment system lost $20 million to criminals between late 2021 and early 2022. In October 2024 the Financial Conduct Authority fined Starling £29 million for weak anti-money-laundering and sanctions controls dating back to 2017. Monzo withdrew its US banking licence application in 2021, and reported its first annual profit for the year to March 2024. Several of these are quality attributes (security, privacy, compliance) that growth outran; see Quality Attributes and Security.

Our view: the speed came from three things together: a regulator that built a staged way in, a narrow first product, and no old systems or branches to carry. Modern technology mattered, but it was one of three, and on its own it would not have produced a bank. The other view: established banks are slower partly by obligation: decades of products, data and customers to keep working, and a scale at which a mistake hurts more people. The fines and incidents above suggest the new banks meet the same pressures as they grow.

Sources: Wikipedia’s articles on challenger banks, Monzo, Revolut and Starling Bank. We could not read the banks’ own engineering blogs from this environment, so this card says nothing about their technology choices.

ISO 20022: the language banks are moving to

  • An ISO standard for electronic data interchange between financial institutions.
  • It is a repository of message and business-process descriptions, modelled in UML and turned into concrete messages (most often XML).
  • Why engineers meet it: payment schemes and correspondent banking are moving their messages to it, so a payments system has to read and produce it, and map it to and from older formats during the move.
  • Why it helps (analysis): structured fields for parties, purposes and references, instead of free text, make screening and reconciliation easier to automate.

Low latency: what “low” means depends on the job

If you are buildingWhat mattersWhat “fast” looks like
A banking app screenThe customer’s wait, every time, including the slow onesTail latency (p99) across every service in the chain, not the average
An instant paymentFinishing every check inside the scheme’s deadlineA whole journey within seconds (SEPA Instant: under ten), with a known budget per step
A market-data or trading systemReacting before the competitionUnder a millisecond is often called “ultra low latency”, and the bar keeps moving

Physics sets the floor. Light in a vacuum covers a kilometre in about 3 microseconds, so a trading engine 150 km from an exchange can never get a round trip under 1 millisecond, before any software runs. Fibre is slower: its core has a refractive index of about 1.4475, which works out (our calculation) at roughly 4.8 microseconds per kilometre, or about 1.4 ms for that round trip. This is why co-location, running servers in the exchange’s own data centre, is one of the defining features of high-frequency trading.

Techniques, and what each one costs: choose by requirement

If this is your problemConsiderWhat it costs you (analysis)
You do not know where the time goesMeasure first: tracing per hop, percentiles, a profiler (see Performance)Time; nothing else on this list is worth doing before it
Database round trips dominateKeep the working state in memory and the database for durability: LMAX’s trading platform runs its business logic in memory with event sourcing, and its business logic processor was reported to handle 6 million orders per second on a single thread, in its team’s own benchmarkRecovery by replaying events; careful design of snapshots
Threads wait on locks and queuesSingle-threaded business logic fed by lock-free ring buffers (LMAX’s Disruptor); fewer hand-offs between threadsA programming model most teams have not used
Garbage-collection pauses on the JVMA low-pause collector: with Generational ZGC (JEP 439), pause times are measured in microseconds; or allocate less on the hot pathSome throughput and memory overhead
Small messages wait in the network stackTurn off Nagle’s algorithm with TCP_NODELAYMore, smaller packets on the network
The kernel’s network stack itself is too slowKernel bypass, such as DPDK, which processes packets entirely in user spaceSpecialised hardware and skills; you take over what the kernel did
Distance dominatesMove closer: same region, same data centre, or co-location at the exchangeMoney, and less freedom over where you run

Our view: most banking systems are slow because of too many network hops and database calls, not because of the garbage collector or the kernel. Work down the table from the top. The other view: in trading, the last rows are the whole game, and teams start there because the first rows were solved long ago.

Measure latency honestly

  • Percentiles, not averages: the slow requests are the ones customers remember (see Performance).
  • Coordinated omission: a load generator that waits for each response before sending the next stops sending exactly when the server is slow, so it quietly misses most of the slow periods. Gil Tene’s wrk2 sends at a constant rate and records into HdrHistogram to avoid it.
  • Measure end to end and per hop: the customer’s wait is the sum of every hop in the chain.
  • Test with production-like data and load, including the batch jobs and peaks of month end.

Correctness under speed

  • Idempotency everywhere: retries are certain at this scale; a repeated payment message must not move money twice (see Payment Gateways).
  • Exactly-once effects, not exactly-once delivery: messages may arrive twice; the ledger must apply them once.
  • Reconcile continuously: compare your ledger with the scheme’s and the bank’s, every day, and treat a mismatch as an incident.
  • Plan for the slow path: when a check cannot finish inside the deadline, decide in advance whether to reject, hold or approve, and record why.

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Explain how channels, core banking, schemes and the ledger fit together
  • Explain why every payment step must be idempotent
  • Read p50, p99 and p99.9 and say which one customers feel

2 Practitioner

  • Build a payment flow that meets an instant scheme's deadline
  • Map a message to and from ISO 20022
  • Load-test without coordinated omission

3 Senior

  • Design for 24/7 operation with no batch window
  • Set and enforce a latency budget per hop
  • Choose between in-memory, lock-free and conventional designs by requirement

4 Expert

  • Lead a core banking or scheme migration
  • Set resilience and latency standards across a bank
  • Design ultra-low-latency trading infrastructure

Each level includes the ones before it. See Start here for what the levels mean.
