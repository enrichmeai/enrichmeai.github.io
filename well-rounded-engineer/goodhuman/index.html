Being a good human

Technical skill decides what you can build; character decides whether people want to build with you, and whether what you build is good for the people who use it. This tab is about the person behind the engineer: honesty, kindness, humility, care for others and for yourself.

Core qualities, in practice

HonestySaying "I broke it", "I don't know yet", "this estimate was wrong" early and plainly
IntegrityDoing the right thing when nobody would notice: the test you could skip, the shortcut with user data

HumilityAssuming you might be wrong; learning from juniors and from mistakes; sharing credit

GratitudeThanking people specifically for what they did

Psychological safety

Teams do their best work when people can ask questions, admit mistakes and disagree without fear. Google's study of its own teams (Project Aristotle) found it was the most important factor in team effectiveness.

  • Admit your own mistakes first; it makes it safe for others.
  • Thank people for raising problems, even when the timing is bad.
  • Ask quieter people for their view.
  • Criticise the work and the system, never the person.
  • Keep blameless postmortems genuinely blameless.

Feedback, given and received

  • Situation, behaviour, impact: "In yesterday's review (situation) you rewrote Asha's query without explaining (behaviour); she didn't learn why and felt sidelined (impact)."
  • Be specific and timely; praise in public, correct in private.
  • Receive feedback with "thank you" first, questions second, defence never.
  • Ask for feedback regularly, not just at review time.

Conflict and disagreement

  • Assume good intent; most conflict is about unclear goals or information.
  • Restate the other view until they agree you understood it.
  • Find the shared goal, then disagree about the route.
  • Use data and small experiments to settle technical disagreements.
  • Disagree and commit: once decided, support the decision fully, or reopen it openly with new evidence.
  • Apologise well: name what you did, its effect, and what you will do differently, with no "but".

Care for users and society

  • Build for everyone: accessibility, other languages, slow networks, older devices.
  • Treat user data as if it were your family's.
  • Be honest in what the product claims; never ship dark patterns that trick people.
  • Think about who could be harmed, not only who benefits.
  • Consider the environmental cost of compute you do not need.

Care for yourself

  • Sustainable pace: long hours produce bugs, poor decisions and burnout. The WHO recognises burnout as an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress.
  • Sleep, movement, breaks: they are part of doing good work, not a reward for finishing it.
  • Boundaries: be reachable when on call; be off when you are off.
  • Ask for help early, technical or personal.
  • Life outside work: family, friends, interests and rest make you a better engineer and a happier person.

Run your day, or the day runs you

If you do not decide how your day starts, the inbox, the chat and whatever is on fire will decide for you. A small routine you keep every day is how you take the day back.

  • Move your body, every day. It does not matter what: a walk, a run, yoga, cycling, a swim, push-ups in the kitchen, dancing, playing with your children. The best exercise is the one you will actually do tomorrow.
  • Start small enough to keep. Twenty minutes you look forward to becomes a habit; an hour you dread is easy to skip. Build the habit first, then make it harder.
  • Anchor it to something you already do: after the first coffee, before you open the laptop, at lunch. A habit tied to a moment survives busy days.
  • Get up from the desk during the day too. Stand, stretch, walk while you talk through a problem: a bug that will not give way at the desk often does on a walk.
  • Protect the basics around it: a regular time to sleep and wake, food that is not only snacks, and a few minutes with no screen.
  • Miss a day, not two. Life will break the routine; just start again the next day, without guilt and without making up for it.

Discipline in small things carries into the work: the engineer who keeps a daily habit is the one who keeps the tests green and the runbook current. If you have a health condition, check with a doctor what kind of movement suits you.

Mentoring and lifting others

  • Explain the why, not just the what.
  • Let people struggle a little before rescuing; then help them reflect.
  • Give stretch work with support.
  • Credit people by name for their work, especially when they are not in the room.
  • Remember who helped you, and pass it on.

A short daily check

  • Was I honest today, including about my mistakes?
  • Did I help someone, and thank someone?
  • Did I listen more than I spoke in at least one conversation?
  • Did I do work I would be proud to have the users see?
  • Did I move my body, and look after my own health?

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Be honest about mistakes and what you do not know
  • Thank people specifically; review kindly
  • Look after your health and ask for help early

2 Practitioner

  • Give and receive feedback well
  • Handle disagreement with respect and evidence
  • Build for all users, including accessibility

3 Senior

  • Create psychological safety in your team
  • Mentor others and share credit generously
  • Raise ethical concerns and stand by them

4 Expert

  • Shape a humane, inclusive culture across the organisation
  • Lead with integrity under pressure
  • Give back to the wider community

Each level includes the ones before it. See Start here for what the levels mean.
