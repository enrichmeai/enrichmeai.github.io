Guide / Security / Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity: thinking like a defender

The Security page is about building secure software. This page is about the wider field: how real attacks unfold, how organisations defend, detect and recover, and the people and roles involved. You do not need to become a specialist, but every engineer should understand the game being played against their systems.

How real attacks unfold

MITRE ATT&CK is a public knowledge base of attackers' tactics and techniques, based on real-world observations. Its Enterprise matrix (version 20.1) groups what attackers do into 15 tactics, roughly in the order an intrusion progresses:

Reconnaissance → Resource Development → Initial Access → Execution → Persistence → Privilege Escalation → Stealth → Defense Impairment → Credential Access → Discovery → Lateral Movement → Collection → Command and Control → Exfiltration → Impact

  • Attackers rarely need anything clever. A stolen password, an unpatched server, a misconfigured bucket or a convincing email is usually enough to start.
  • Every step is a chance to stop them. Defence is not one wall; it is noticing and blocking at many of these stages.
  • Use it practically: for each system you own, ask which techniques would work against it, and whether you would see them.

A brief history of attacks, and what everyone should do

Security is not only a job for specialists. Look at how some of the best-known attacks got in, and most began with something ordinary: software not updated, a message someone trusted, a password with nothing behind it, or a supplier everyone relied on. Those are things everyone can do something about.

YearAttackHow it got inWhat it did
1988Morris wormHoles in common Unix programs (sendmail and finger); it copied itself from machine to machine across the early internetOne of the oldest worms spread over the internet, and the first US felony conviction under the 1986 Computer Fraud and Abuse Act
2000ILOVEYOUAn email attachment that seemed to come from someone you knew, then mailed itself to everyone in your address bookInfected an estimated 10% of internet-connected computers; about $10 billion of damage
2003SQL SlammerA bug in Microsoft SQL Server whose fix had been released six months earlier, but not appliedMost of its 75,000 victims were infected within 10 minutes
2010StuxnetTypically an infected USB drive, crossing into machines that were not on the internet; four previously unknown flawsDamaged equipment in Iran’s nuclear programme: code causing physical harm
2014HeartbleedA missing length check in OpenSSL, a library much of the web uses for encryption; in the code since 2012A fix came out the day it was disclosed; every site using the library had to update
2017WannaCryA Windows flaw Microsoft had patched on 14 March 2017; the attack began on 12 May 2017Encrypted files on more than 300,000 computers in 150 countries and demanded bitcoin
2017EquifaxA website still running an old version of Apache Struts with a known flawRecords of 147.9 million Americans, 15.2 million British and about 19,000 Canadian citizens
2020SolarWindsA widely used IT management product, compromised in the supply chain, then installed by its customersThousands of organisations, including parts of the US federal government; undetected for months
2021Colonial PipelineA compromised password for an inactive VPN account that did not have multi-factor authenticationThe company halted its fuel pipeline to contain the ransomware, and paid the ransom
2021Log4ShellA flaw in Log4j, a Java logging library used almost everywhere; unnoticed since 2013Rated 10, the highest CVSS score; potentially hundreds of millions of devices
2024XZ Utils backdoorA backdoor added on purpose to open-source compression software by a contributor using the name “Jia Tan”Found by a developer, Andres Freund, before the affected versions were widely deployed to production systems; rated 10.0

The pattern (analysis): four ways in keep coming back. Unpatched software (Slammer, WannaCry, Equifax), where the fix existed before the attack. People tricked (ILOVEYOU, and phishing in general). Passwords alone (Colonial Pipeline). Trusted suppliers and shared code (SolarWinds, Heartbleed, Log4Shell, XZ Utils), where one weak link reaches everyone who uses it.

HabitWhat to doWhich attacks it would have helped against
Multi-factor sign-inTurn on multi-factor sign-in (a code, an app prompt or a security key, as well as the password), most of all for email, banking and work accountsColonial Pipeline: one password was enough
Unique passwordsUse a different password everywhere, kept in a password manager, so one leaked password does not open your other accountsLeaked passwords: Wikipedia says the one used at Colonial Pipeline was likely found on the dark web
UpdatesInstall updates promptly, and turn on automatic updates for your devices and appsSQL Slammer, WannaCry and Equifax: fixes were available beforehand
Check the unexpectedBe suspicious of the unexpected: an attachment, a link, an urgent request for money or a password. Check by another route (call the person, open the site yourself) before actingILOVEYOU, and phishing generally
BackupsKeep backups, with at least one copy that is not always connectedRansomware such as WannaCry. Wikipedia cites a 2020 survey in which 56% of ransomware victims got their data back from backups, against 26% who paid; newer ransomware also threatens to leak data, which backups do not prevent
Remove the unusedRemove what you no longer use: old accounts, apps, browser extensions, devices still signed inColonial Pipeline: the account was inactive
Unknown USB drivesDo not plug in unknown USB drives, and do not lend yours to unknown machinesStuxnet crossed into machines that were not on the internet this way
Report quicklyReport quickly if you clicked something, or something feels wrong. Early reporting is what limits the damage, and nobody should be blamed for itSolarWinds stayed hidden for months

If you build software, the same four ways in apply to your systems: keep dependencies updated and know what you ship (Quality Attributes, the vulnerability loop), check access on every object, and never log secrets (Security, and the security drills in the labs).

Our view: most of the damage in this history came through ordinary doors that ordinary habits close, so the basics above are worth more to most people than any specialist tool. The other view: some attacks, such as Stuxnet or the XZ backdoor, were built by well-resourced attackers that no personal habit would stop; defending against them is the work of vendors, open-source maintainers and governments, and blaming individuals for those would be unfair.

Sources: Wikipedia’s articles on each attack in the table, and on multi-factor authentication, phishing and ransomware. We could not read the UK NCSC’s or the US CISA’s advice pages from this environment.

Defence in depth

  • Identity first: strong, phishing-resistant sign-in, least privilege, and removing access people no longer need.
  • Keep things patched and known: you cannot protect what you do not know you have. Keep an inventory of systems, software and data.
  • Segment: a breach in one place should not open everything else.
  • Assume breach: design so that one compromised laptop, account or service does limited damage.
  • Back up, offline: see "Backups and ransomware" on the Security page.

Detect and respond

  • Collect the right signals: sign-ins, admin actions, process and network activity, cloud audit logs.
  • Detection is a craft: write rules for behaviour attackers cannot easily avoid, test them, and tune out noise, so real alerts are not drowned.
  • Have an incident plan before you need it: who leads, who decides, how you contain (isolate a machine, revoke a credential), how you communicate, and when you call in help or the authorities.
  • Learn without blame afterwards, and fix the root cause, not just the symptom.

The people and roles

  • Blue team / security operations (SOC): watch, detect and respond.
  • Red team and penetration testers: attack, with written permission, to find weaknesses first.
  • Purple teaming: both sides together, turning each attack into a detection.
  • Application and cloud security engineers: help builders get it right from the start (see Security).
  • Governance, risk and compliance: policies, audits and standards (see "Compliance frameworks" on the Security page).
  • Threat intelligence: who is attacking whom, and how.

Ethics and the law

  • Only test systems you own, or have written permission to test. Scanning or probing someone else's systems without permission is illegal in many countries, whatever your intent.
  • Practise legally: deliberately vulnerable training apps, capture-the-flag events and your own home lab.
  • Report what you find responsibly: through the organisation's security contact or vulnerability disclosure programme, and give them time to fix it.

Security is a team sport. Our view: much of the defence is ordinary engineers who patch, review, log, ask "what if this is stolen?", and report mistakes quickly.

Judging a cloud provider’s security: the Cloud Security Alliance

The Cloud Security Alliance (CSA) is a not-for-profit organisation, formed in 2008, that promotes security best practice for cloud computing. Three of its outputs come up whenever a team chooses or audits a cloud service:

NameWhat it is
Cloud Controls Matrix (CCM)A framework of security controls for cloud providers and their customers, grouped into domains. Providers use it to evaluate and document their controls.
Consensus Assessments Initiative Questionnaire (CAIQ)Questions built on the CCM that a customer or auditor may ask a provider. A provider answers it to document how it complies with the CSA’s best practices. In CCM version 4 the questionnaire and the matrix were combined.
STAR (Security, Trust, Assurance and Risk) registryA free public registry, launched in 2013 with the British Standards Institution, where providers publish their CSA assessments.
  • STAR Level 1, self-assessment: the provider publishes a completed CAIQ or a report documenting its compliance with the CCM. It is free and open to any provider.
  • STAR Level 2, third-party assessment: an independent auditor checks the controls. STAR Certification builds on the ISO/IEC 27001 management system standard plus the CCM; STAR Attestation is a SOC 2 Type 2 audit with the CCM’s criteria added.
  • Continuous monitoring: Google describes STAR as a three-step programme of self-assessment, third-party audit and continuous monitoring. One Microsoft page calls this Level 3; two other Microsoft pages list only two levels.

The matrix, the questionnaire and even the number of levels differ between sources: Microsoft’s own pages give 16 or 17 CCM domains, “more than 140” or “more than 250” CAIQ questions, and two or three STAR levels, so check which version an assessment used. Analysis: for an engineer, a provider’s STAR entry answers part of a supplier review without a new questionnaire, and the CCM is a checklist for your own side of the shared responsibility too. A self-assessment is the provider’s own word; a Level 2 report is an auditor’s, and its scope (which services and regions) is what we would check first. The other way to look at it: a detailed Level 1 answer on the exact control you care about can tell you more than a broad Level 2 certificate that does not cover your service.

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Explain how a typical intrusion unfolds
  • Spot phishing and report incidents quickly
  • Know that you only test systems you may test

2 Practitioner

  • Map your system's exposure to common attacker techniques
  • Log the signals an investigation needs
  • Follow the incident plan during an incident

3 Senior

  • Write and tune detections; run a tabletop exercise
  • Lead containment and recovery in an incident
  • Design defence in depth for a product

4 Expert

  • Own the organisation's security operations and incident response
  • Run red, blue and purple team programmes
  • Build a security culture across teams

Each level includes the ones before it. See Start here for what the levels mean.
