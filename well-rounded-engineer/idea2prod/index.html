Guide / Idea to Production

From idea to production, with people and agents

Taking an idea all the way to something people use in production is the whole job, and AI agents now take part in every step of it. This page walks the path once, stage by stage: what each stage is for, what people own, how agents can help, and which page of this guide goes deeper. It ends with two views on living with the change, and on the foundations that keep you useful through it.

The short answer

Our view: the path from idea to production has not changed in shape: find out whether the idea is worth building, build it in small steps, check it, release it safely, run it, and learn. What has changed is how much of the building agents can do. That moves people’s value towards the ends of the path, deciding what is worth doing and judging whether it worked, and towards the foundations that let you check an agent’s work. The other view is in the cards below: some expect the division of work, and even the shape of the path, to keep shifting.

A checklist that has lasted

Google’s SRE book prints the company’s original Launch Coordination Checklist, from about 2005. Its headings are still a fair list of what production asks of an idea: architecture; machines and data centres; volume, capacity and performance; reliability and failover; monitoring; security; automation and manual tasks; growth; external dependencies; and schedule and rollout. The book says checklists like it are used to reduce failure and ensure consistency, as in aviation and surgery.

Analysis: an agent can work through such a list quickly; deciding which answers are good enough for your users is still a person’s call.

One ecosystem, not two camps

Our view: people and agents work best as one system with clear roles: people set direction, own decisions and stay accountable; agents do well-briefed work quickly and report what they did and how they checked it. The guide’s Humans & Agents page has how to brief an agent and how it should report back.

The other view: some argue roles will keep moving towards agents, so drawing fixed lines now may age badly; on that view the skill that matters most is adapting the division of work as tools change.

In harmony with what is happening

Our view: the change is real and it is not stopping, and fighting it costs energy that learning would use better. Being in harmony with it does not mean accepting everything: it means seeing clearly what is changing, keeping what still matters, and choosing where to put your effort. The guide’s Philosophy page has more on nothing being permanent, and on being at peace with what happens; Worried about AI? has more on the worry itself.

The other view: some changes deserve resistance, not harmony: when a tool is unsafe, unfair or simply wrong for the job, saying so is part of the work, and acceptance can become passivity.

Upskilling on foundations

Our view: when agents write more of the code, the foundations matter more, not less: you cannot judge an agent’s database migration without understanding transactions, or its retry logic without understanding failure. The foundations change slowly; the tools on top of them change every year. That is why this guide starts where it does, and why Start here argues for being the site manager rather than a “vibe coder”.
