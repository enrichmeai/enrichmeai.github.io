Why this guide exists

A short story, and a promise: a click is cheaper than a prompt.

Where I started

I started as an electronics engineer, moved into computer hardware, and then into software. When I began, it was Windows 98, and we booted machines from floppy disks. Memory was scarce: we worked with 128 MB of RAM, and 256 MB was a luxury. In my first software job, at one of the smallest startups you can imagine, our internet came over a dial-up line.

Java, as I remember it, was at version 1.1. We could not download the documentation, so we used javap to read a class's methods and worked out the rest. We wrote code in Notepad. Then we found EditPlus, wired it to javac and java so we could compile and run from the editor, and the coloured code alone made us feel we had arrived.

Learning from the platform up

We learned core Java, then servlets and applets, and within two years we were building web applications. Struts arrived around then: an Apache project, founded in May 2000 by Craig McClanahan (started, as I remember it, as a holiday project). Hans Bergsten's writing on JSP was how many of us learned, and I remember puzzling over the difference between the servlet context and the page context.

Browsers were not standard. What we now call Ajax worked through Microsoft's XMLHTTP object in Internet Explorer, so we wrote separate code for Internet Explorer and Netscape. There was no Chrome.

The EJB years

At one of the biggest retail software companies of the time, we built with Enterprise JavaBeans: stateless session beans to read data, entity beans for the database, and, in booking systems of that era, stateful session beans holding a conversation while the database held the lock on a ticket. XSLT generated our screens from data, and UML and model-driven architecture promised that drawing the model would write the code.

We generated code long before AI

Code generation is not new. I used database metadata to generate reusable boilerplate code, much as people use AI today, and XSLT templates to turn data into dynamic screens. We generated the JavaScript for tree structures, pagination and web-based tutorials.

The lesson has stayed with me: generating code is easy; understanding what you generate is the job.

The framework years

Enterprise Java was hard. We wrestled with CORBA and its interface definitions, with two-phase commits for transactions that spanned systems, and later with enterprise service buses. Then came Spring, which its own documentation says came into being in 2003 as a response to the complexity of the early J2EE specifications. Rod Johnson had argued for simpler designs in his book Expert One-on-One J2EE Design and Development (Wrox), which is still on my shelf; as I remember it, his ideas about plain Java objects were at Spring's heart, and after VMware took the company over, Spring became the standard choice. For many of us, it started with a single core jar.

Something changed in those years. Many engineers learned the frameworks without ever looking at a servlet. The frameworks were good, but the foundations underneath them became invisible.

My bookshelf

The first technical book I read cover to cover was Hunter and Crawford's Java Servlet Programming (O'Reilly). My first UML tool was MagicDraw.

At my first startup, from 2000 to 2002, I could not afford Enterprise Java with UML (Arrington and Rayhan, Wiley). When I could, in 2003, I bought it for myself, out of love for it: it explained every layer, why DTOs exist, and when a plain hash map is enough. Seeing why each layer is there is what today's polished frameworks and generated answers rarely show.

Beside them still: Java Message Service (Monson-Haefel and Chappell), Java Enterprise Best Practices, JavaServer Pages and Rod Johnson's Expert One-on-One J2EE Design and Development, packed away for a house move and going back on a shelf soon.

What worries me now

I do not want to generalise, but I see a newer version of the same pattern. Some new engineers go straight to AI without learning the fundamentals, or even the frameworks. They can get an answer, but they cannot always tell whether it is right, or fix it when it breaks.

At the same time, much of the simple knowledge people once found on a website, and followed through themselves, is now asked of an AI, one question at a time. Every one of those questions costs tokens, energy and data-centre capacity, often for something a page could answer with a click.

Why this guide is free

I would rather spend my own tokens once, building something useful, than have thousands of people spend theirs asking for the same basics. So this guide is free, and so are the frameworks we build: Culvert, Penstock, Cistern and the others on the home page, all open source.

A click is cheaper than a prompt. For what is already known, read the tab. Use AI where it earns its keep: complex business logic, building frameworks, investigating a hard problem, or when you genuinely need to move fast. AI is here to stay; use it in the right context.

And keep the foundations. They are what let you tell a good answer from one that only looks good.
