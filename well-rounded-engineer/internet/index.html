How the internet is tied together

The internet is not one network. It is tens of thousands of networks agreeing to carry each other's traffic, held together by a few shared systems. When one of those systems fails, much of the web goes down at once. Read Networking first.

The pieces

PieceWhat it does
Autonomous systemsEach internet provider, cloud or large company runs its own network, an autonomous system, with its own block of addresses.
BGP (Border Gateway Protocol)How autonomous systems tell each other which addresses they can reach. It is the internet's map, and every network trusts the others to describe it honestly.
Internet exchange pointsBuildings full of switches where many networks connect directly and exchange traffic, instead of sending it the long way round.
Submarine cablesFibre-optic cables on the sea bed carry the great majority of data between continents.
DNS and the root serversDNS turns names into addresses. At its top are thirteen well-known root server addresses, served by many copies around the world.
Content delivery networksAkamai, Cloudflare and others keep copies of websites close to users and absorb attacks, so a large share of the web passes through them.
Cloud regionsHuge data centres run by a few providers, where much of today's software and data live. See How the cloud is built.

When a shared piece fails

  • 4 October 2021, BGP: Facebook withdrew the routes to its own name servers in a burst of BGP updates, and its services were unavailable worldwide for six to seven hours.
  • 20 October 2025, a cloud region: services in AWS's Northern Virginia region (us-east-1) failed, taking down sites such as Roblox, Fortnite, Snapchat and Duolingo.
  • 18 November 2025, a CDN: a Cloudflare outage took down major platforms, including X and ChatGPT.

Each one is a single shared dependency that many companies chose independently. See Kubernetes DR and SRE on designing for it.

What engineers can do

  • Know your dependencies: DNS provider, CDN, cloud region, identity provider, payment gateway. Write them down.
  • Avoid one region for everything that must stay up; test failing over before you need it.
  • Lower DNS TTLs before planned changes, and keep a way to switch providers.
  • Show an honest status when a dependency is down, rather than a confusing error. See Observability.

Levels: what to expect

1 Foundation

  • Explain that the internet is many networks connected together
  • Explain what DNS, a CDN and a cloud region are
  • List your service's outside dependencies

2 Practitioner

  • Trace a request's path with traceroute and DNS lookups
  • Explain BGP and why a bad route can take a company offline
  • Configure a CDN and DNS for a service

3 Senior

  • Design for the failure of a DNS provider, CDN or region
  • Plan multi-region and multi-provider set-ups
  • Run outage drills for shared dependencies

4 Expert

  • Own internet-facing architecture and its resilience
  • Negotiate peering and provider strategy
  • Lead response to large external outages

Each level includes the ones before it. See Start here for what the levels mean.
